Compressors for process engineers

How each type works and where it fits, how to calculate head, power and discharge temperature, how to read a compressor map and a vendor datasheet, which API, ASME and ISO standards apply, and how to keep a compressor from hurting anyone. Nine worked examples and eighteen problems to solve.

Alireza Lotfollahzade, EIT Updated 11 November 2026 About 100 minutes to read, longer with the problems SI units, absolute pressures (bara); compressor maps on this page are illustrative

A compressor is a pump for a fluid that shrinks as you push it. That one difference makes it heat the gas, makes its performance depend on what the gas is, and gives every centrifugal machine a minimum flow below which it surges and can wreck itself in seconds.

This page is for chemical and process engineers who size compressors, fill in datasheets, check vendor proposals and sit in HAZOPs. It starts with the thermodynamics, works through the types, staging, reciprocating and centrifugal performance, surge control and vendor data, and ends with standards, safety and a set of problems. If you can solve the problems without opening the solutions, you can specify, check and troubleshoot most process compressors.

Convert standard flow to actual flow, pick a type for a duty, calculate polytropic and isentropic head, discharge temperature and power, choose the number of stages, estimate the capacity of a reciprocating cylinder, read a compressor map and a vendor datasheet, predict what a change in gas does to a centrifugal machine, size an anti-surge recycle and a settle-out pressure, and name the safeguards a HAZOP will ask for.

01

Gas is not a liquid: flow, ratio and head

A pump moves a liquid whose density hardly changes. A compressor works on a gas whose density changes with every bar and every kelvin:

ρ = P M / (Z R T)(1)

P absolute pressure (Pa), M molar mass (kg/kmol), Z compressibility factor (–), R = 8,314 J/kmol·K, T absolute temperature (K).

That gives you three different flows for the same stream, and mixing them up is the most common error in compressor work:

  • Mass flow (kg/h) is conserved. It comes out of your heat and material balance.
  • Standard flow (Sm³/h, MMSCFD) is a molar flow in disguise: the volume the gas would fill at a reference temperature and pressure.
  • Actual flow (m³/h at flange conditions) is the volume the machine physically swallows. Cylinders are sized on it and centrifugal maps are drawn against it.
Q1 = ṅ Z1 R T1 / P1 = Qstd × (Pstd/P1) × (T1/Tstd) × (Z1/Zstd)(2)

Example 1 turns 1.2 million Sm³/d at 30 bara into 1,650 m³/h at the compressor inlet, a factor of 30. Give a vendor a standard flow without composition and suction conditions and you have told them nothing.

The pressure ratio r = P2/P1 uses absolute pressures. A ratio of gauge pressures is meaningless.

The head is the work done on each kilogram of gas, in kJ/kg. It plays the same role as pump head: a given centrifugal impeller at a given speed and flow puts roughly the same head into any gas. A heavy gas turns that head into a large pressure ratio and a light gas into a small one, which is why hydrogen-rich recycle compressors need so many impellers and why a change in gas composition moves a centrifugal compressor around its map.

Finally, compression heats the gas. Almost all the work ends up as enthalpy, and the discharge temperature, not the pressure, is usually what limits how much ratio one stage can take.

Standard conditions are not standard. 15 °C and 1.01325 bar (ISO, used on this page), 60 °F and 14.696 psia (US gas industry, 1 MMSCFD ≈ 1,177 Sm³/h at 15 °C), and 0 °C for "normal" Nm³. Sm³ and Nm³ differ by 5.5 %. Write the reference on every datasheet.

Head in metres. 60 kJ/kg is 60,000 m²/s², or 6.1 km of "gas column". Older US literature quotes ft·lbf/lbm; 1 kJ/kg = 334.6 ft·lbf/lbm.

02

Types of compressors and how they work

There are two families, and almost everything on this page follows from the difference between them.

Positive displacement machines trap a volume of gas and make it smaller. Their flow is set by speed and geometry and barely changes with discharge pressure: block the outlet and they keep compressing until something gives, so each needs a relief valve. They have no surge.

Dynamic machines give the gas velocity with rotating blades and turn that velocity into pressure in a diffuser. The head they can make is set by blade tip speed, so their flow is decided by the system they push against. Below a minimum flow they surge, and above a maximum flow they choke.

Ejectors sit outside both families: a high-pressure motive fluid entrains and compresses the suction gas with no moving parts.

How each one works

Reciprocating. A crankshaft drives a crosshead, piston rod and piston in a cylinder. Self-acting suction and discharge valves (plate, ring or poppet) open on pressure difference alone. Double-acting cylinders compress on both sides of the piston. A distance piece and packing keep process gas out of the crankcase. A small clearance volume is left at the end of each stroke; its gas re-expands and costs capacity (section 6). Recips cover the widest range of gases, from hydrogen (M = 2) to chlorine (M = 70), at about 3 per stage, with up to six stages and overall ratios above 300.2 Process machines to API 618 run slowly, typically 275–600 rpm.2

Screw. Two meshing helical rotors trap gas in the grooves and carry it along the axis while the pocket shrinks. In an oil-flooded screw the injected oil seals the clearances, lubricates and absorbs heat, so one stage can take a large ratio; the oil must be separated from the gas downstream and must not be diluted by it. A dry screw uses timing gears to keep the rotors apart and is limited by discharge temperature. Screws have no surge, a wide turndown, and dry screws tolerate dirty gas.2 Mechanical limits on differential pressure are about 12 bar for oil-free and 20 bar for oil-flooded machines.3

Lobe, vane and liquid ring. Lobe blowers move pockets of gas without internal compression and stop at ratios near 2. Sliding-vane machines have vanes riding in slots of an eccentric rotor. In a liquid-ring machine a rotating ring of seal liquid forms the pistons; compression is nearly isothermal and the machine swallows condensate and dirt, but its efficiency is low.

Diaphragm. A hydraulically flexed metal diaphragm compresses the gas, which never touches oil or a sliding seal: the choice for very pure, toxic or high-pressure hydrogen duties at small flows.

Centrifugal. Each impeller flings gas outward, adding velocity and pressure; a diffuser turns the velocity into more pressure, and a return channel guides the gas to the next impeller. A balance piston offsets axial thrust and shaft seals (today mostly dry gas seals) contain the gas. Tip speed and Mach number cap the head per impeller, so a casing holds several impellers. Horizontally split casings are used below about 60 bar operating pressure; barrel (radially split) casings above that, and whenever hydrogen partial pressure at maximum allowable working pressure exceeds 13.8 bar.2

Integrally geared. A bull gear drives one or more pinions with an impeller on each end. Each pinion runs at the speed that suits its impellers, and the gas can be cooled after every stage, which makes these machines efficient for air and CO₂.4 API 617 has covered them in a dedicated part since the 7th edition of 2002.5

Axial. Rows of rotating and stationary blades add a little pressure each. They handle the largest flows at high efficiency, with a narrower stable range than centrifugal machines.

Stage, section, casing. In a centrifugal machine a stage is one impeller. A section is a group of impellers between an inlet and an outlet nozzle, for example between intercoolers. A casing is the pressure-containing body. In a recip, a stage is one cylinder (or set of parallel cylinders) between coolers.

Oil-free gas comes from dry screws, centrifugals, non-lubricated recips and diaphragm machines. Specify it when oil would poison a catalyst, foul a cold box or contaminate a product.

Inside the overlaps, the gas and the operation decide. A few rules from a contractor's selection guide are worth keeping:2

  • A centrifugal compressor is the default wherever the conditions allow, despite its higher capital cost, because it runs for years between overhauls.
  • Below about 170 m³/h of inlet flow, check with vendors before assuming a centrifugal; few build that small.
  • Avoid more than three casings in one drive train.
  • For plant air, an oil-flooded screw with oil-removal filters suits flows up to about 3,800 Nm³/h, a dry screw about 3,500 Nm³/h, and integrally geared centrifugals above that. The latter need blow-off below about 70–80 % capacity, which wastes power at turndown.

A selection sequence

  1. Fix mass flow, composition, suction pressure and temperature for every case: normal, rated, minimum, start-up, regeneration and each credible alternative gas.
  2. Convert to actual inlet flow with equation (2) and calculate the overall ratio and head (section 4).
  3. Screen with Figure 3 and Table 1.
  4. Set the number of stages from the discharge temperature limit (section 5).
  5. Check turndown and control: centrifugals need recycle near surge, recips unload in steps, screws use slide valves or speed.
  6. Check the gas: liquids need scrubbers, dirty or polymerizing gas points to dry screws or liquid rings, and an oil-free requirement removes lubricated machines.
  7. Decide sparing. Centrifugals are often unspared because they run for years; recips are often installed as 2 × 100 % or 3 × 50 % because valves and packing need regular maintenance.

04

Thermodynamics and the core equations

Three idealized paths bracket every real compression.

Isothermal compression, with perfect cooling, needs the least work. No machine achieves it, but it is the yardstick for intercooling:

Wiso = (Z R T1/M) ln r(3)

Isentropic (reversible adiabatic) compression is the reference used for reciprocating and screw machines:

Hs = Zavg (R T1/M) · k/(k − 1) · [r(k−1)/k − 1](4)

Polytropic compression follows P vn = constant with a fixed small-step efficiency ηp. It is the reference for centrifugal and axial machines:

Hp = Zavg (R T1/M) · n/(n − 1) · [r(n−1)/n − 1](5)
(n − 1)/n = (k − 1) / (k ηp)(6)

The discharge temperature follows from either definition:

T2 = T1 r(n−1)/n   or   T2 = T1 [1 + (r(k−1)/k − 1)/ηs](7)

and both routes give the same gas power and shaft power:

Pgas = ṁ Hp/ηp = ṁ Hs/ηs    Pshaft = Pgas + mechanical losses(8)

The two efficiencies describe the same machine, but they are not equal. For r > 1 the isentropic efficiency is always the lower one, and the gap grows with ratio:

ηs = [r(k−1)/k − 1] / [r(k−1)/(k ηp) − 1](9)

Example 2 shows 80 % polytropic is 78.9 % isentropic at a ratio of 1.58. At a ratio of 8, the same 80 % polytropic is only about 74–75 % isentropic. Quote efficiencies without saying which kind and you are a few percent wrong on power before you start.

Why do centrifugal people prefer polytropic? Because the polytropic efficiency of a well-designed impeller hardly depends on its ratio, so stages can be stacked and compared. Isentropic efficiency falls as stages are added, even if every stage is equally good.

Real gases

Equations (4) and (5) assume an ideal gas corrected with an average Z. With Zavg = (Z1 + Z2)/2 and k at average conditions, they stay within about 1 % of a rigorous calculation for most hydrocarbon and air duties; Example 9 compares them for CO₂ up to 30 bara and finds 0.5 % on head. Near the critical point, in the dense phase or at very high ratios, use an equation of state (Peng–Robinson, BWRS or GERG-2008) in your simulator.

ASME PTC 10 and ISO 5389 define polytropic work using the methods John Schultz published in 1962, with compressibility functions X and Y.8 Those methods lose accuracy near the critical point and in the dense phase.9 The 2022 revision of PTC 10, its first since 1997, moved to real-gas relations, a polytropic process throughout, and new methods for polytropic head and efficiency.10 If a vendor's head disagrees with yours by more than 1–2 %, ask which method and equation of state they used before you argue about efficiency.

Units. With R = 8.314 kJ/kmol·K and M in kg/kmol, R/M is in kJ/kg·K and every head on this page comes out in kJ/kg.

Which k? Use k = cp/cv at suction for a first estimate, and the average of suction and discharge when the ratio is large. Typical values: H₂ 1.41, air 1.40, methane 1.30, natural gas 1.25–1.30, CO₂ 1.29, propane 1.13.

Mechanical losses (bearings, seals, gears) are a few percent of gas power. The examples here assume 2 %.

Staging, intercooling and the compression calculator

Three things limit how much ratio one stage can take.

Discharge temperature. API 618 limits the predicted discharge temperature of reciprocating cylinders to 150 °C unless otherwise specified, and to 135 °C for hydrogen-rich gas (M ≤ 12), with lower limits for high-pressure hydrogen and non-lubricated cylinders.2 Above those values lubricating oil cokes, valves and rings wear fast and, in air service, carbon deposits can ignite (section 12). Olefins, dienes and some sulfur compounds foul or polymerize at lower temperatures still.

Mechanics. Rod load in a recip, rotor deflection and differential pressure in a screw, and tip speed and Mach number in a centrifugal impeller.

Power. Cooling the gas between stages brings the path closer to isothermal. For an ideal gas cooled back to the same temperature, total power is lowest when every stage has the same ratio:

rstage = roverall1/N(10)

In practice the designer shifts a little ratio away from the last stages to allow for intercooler and scrubber pressure drops and for the rise in Z, but equation (10) is the right starting point. The calculator below applies it; Figure 4 shows what each extra stage buys.

Diminishing returns. Going from one to two stages on 1 → 8 bar air saves 18 % of the power; the third stage saves another 6 %; the sixth stage saves almost nothing (Example 3 and Figure 4). Every stage adds a cooler, a scrubber, piping and pressure drop.

    Reciprocating compressors

    Displacement, clearance and volumetric efficiency

    The piston sweeps a fixed volume every stroke. For a double-acting cylinder of bore area A, rod area a, stroke L and speed N:

    PD = (2A − a) · L · N   (single-acting head end: A · L · N)(11)

    Not all of that volume fills with fresh gas. At the end of the discharge stroke the clearance volume (valve pockets and the gap between piston and head) stays full of gas at discharge pressure. On the return stroke that gas re-expands, and the suction valves open only when the cylinder pressure falls below suction pressure. Figure 5 shows the cycle. The usual estimate of volumetric efficiency, in percent, is:11

    VE = 100 − r − C [ (Zs/Zd) r1/k − 1 ](12)

    C is the clearance as a percent of displacement. The "− r" term is an allowance for valve losses, leakage and heating; some vendors use 96 in place of 100.11 Capacity at suction conditions = PD × VE.12

    Two consequences matter in practice. Clearance hurts more at high ratio and with low-k gases, and at a high enough ratio the cylinder delivers nothing at all. And adding clearance is a cheap way to cut capacity: that is what a clearance pocket does.

    Typical clearance is 10–20 % for process cylinders. High-pressure cylinders with small bores have more, because valve pockets do not shrink with the bore.

    Ratio where delivery stops: setting the ideal VE to zero gives rmax = (1 + 100/C)k.

    Capacity control

    • Suction valve unloaders hold the suction valves of one cylinder end open, so that end pushes gas back and forth without compressing it. Load steps of 100, 75, 50, 25 and 0 % are typical on a two-cylinder stage.
    • Clearance pockets, fixed or variable, add volume to the head end and lower VE through equation (12).
    • Speed through a variable-frequency drive or an engine governor.
    • Stepless systems hold the suction valves open for part of each stroke, giving smooth control with little wasted work.
    • Recycle from discharge, after the cooler, back to suction. Always available, always wasteful.

    Unloading one end of a cylinder changes the interstage pressures of a multistage machine, which shifts ratio, temperature and rod load onto other stages. Ask the vendor for a load-step table that shows all of them.

    Rod load and rod reversal

    The gas pushes on both sides of a double-acting piston. With gauge pressures, the gas load on the rod when the head end is compressing is approximately:

    Fgas = Pd A − Ps (A − a)(13)

    The frame, crosshead and rod are rated for a maximum allowable load, and the vendor checks it at every load step and at relief-valve set pressure. The combined load (gas plus inertia) must also reverse once per revolution so that oil can flow into the crosshead pin bushing. API 618 asks for the reversal to last at least 15° of crank angle with a peak at least 3 % of the load in the opposite direction.13 Unloading the head end of a cylinder can remove the reversal; that is a common cause of wiped crosshead bushings.

    Pulsation and vibration

    Every valve opening sends a pressure pulse into the piping. Unchecked, pulsations shake piping and fatigue small-bore connections, and they cost capacity and power. API 618 sets three levels of pulsation and vibration design: Design Approach 1 relies on empirical bottle sizing, Design Approach 2 adds an acoustic simulation of the piping, and Design Approach 3 adds a mechanical response analysis.14 The level is chosen from discharge pressure and power per cylinder.15 Design Approach 1 was removed in the 6th edition of 2024.16 The process engineer's part is to give the analyst every operating case, including start-up and every load step, because a system that is quiet at full load can resonate at 50 %.

    Distance pieces. The space between the crankcase and the cylinder packing. For toxic or flammable gas, specify a two-compartment distance piece, purged and vented to a safe location, so packing leakage cannot reach the crankcase or the operator.

    Non-lubricated cylinders use PTFE-based rings for oil-free gas, at lower piston speeds and discharge temperatures.

    Centrifugal compressors and the compressor map

    A centrifugal impeller with backward-leaning blades makes the most head at low flow and less as flow rises. Plot polytropic head against actual inlet flow for several speeds and you have the compressor map of Figure 1. Each speed line ends on the left at the surge line and on the right at the stonewall (choke), where the gas reaches sonic velocity in the narrowest passage and head and efficiency collapse.

    API 617 and vendors use a few defined terms that you will meet on every proposal:1718

    • Rated point: the point on the 100 % speed curve at the highest flow of any specified operating point. It is usually derived, not an actual operating case.
    • Normal point: where the machine is expected to run most of the time and where the best efficiency is wanted.
    • Stability: the flow range from the rated point to surge at rated speed, as a percent of rated flow.
    • Turndown: the flow range from rated to surge at rated head (by changing speed), as a percent of rated flow.
    • Rise to surge: the increase in head from the rated point to surge at rated speed. A steeper curve controls better and resists surge.

    For the illustrative machine in Figure 1 the stability is 35 %, the turndown 37 % and the rise to surge 8 %.

    Fan laws

    For the same gas and suction conditions, moving to a new speed shifts each point along a parabola through the origin:

    Q2/Q1 = N2/N1    H2/H1 = (N2/N1)²    P2/P1 = (N2/N1)³(14)

    They hold well for modest speed changes. Over large changes, Mach number effects and the shifting volume ratio between the first and last impellers make them approximate.

    What the gas does to the machine

    To a first approximation, the head-flow curve in kJ/kg against actual inlet flow does not depend on the gas. The pressure ratio does: equation (5) turns a fixed head into a larger ratio for a heavier, colder or more compressible gas. Most centrifugal operating problems come from forgetting this.1920

    Why "actual inlet flow"? Because the impeller sees volume, not mass. Plotting head against actual inlet flow keeps the map nearly the same for different gases, which is why vendors draw it that way.

    Maximum continuous speed is normally at least 105 % of the highest speed any specified case needs; the driver trip speed sits above it.

    Capacity control, from best to worst

    1. Variable speed (steam or gas turbine, VFD motor) moves the whole speed line and wastes little power.
    2. Adjustable inlet guide vanes pre-swirl the gas to lower the head at constant speed; common on air machines, with a turndown of about 30 %.18
    3. Suction throttling lowers the inlet density, so the machine handles less mass at the same actual flow. Simple and less wasteful than discharge throttling.
    4. Discharge throttling pushes the machine up its curve toward surge. Avoid it.
    5. Recycle or blow-off keeps the compressor's own flow up while the process takes less. It is surge protection, not a control strategy for long-term turndown.

    08

    Surge and anti-surge control

    Reduce the flow through a centrifugal compressor far enough and the impellers and diffusers stall. The machine can no longer hold the head that the downstream system holds against it, so the flow reverses for a moment, the discharge pressure falls, forward flow resumes, and the cycle repeats. Each cycle reverses the axial thrust and heats the gas, and repeated surge damages thrust bearings, seals and impellers.21 A compressor can be destroyed in a few cycles, which is why anti-surge protection is a safety function, not a refinement.

    An anti-surge system measures inlet flow, suction and discharge pressure and temperature, calculates how far the operating point is from the surge line, and opens a fast recycle valve when the point reaches a surge control line set a margin to the right of the surge line, typically 10 % in flow.1 Some designs use 3–10 %, trading a smaller margin for less recycle.21 The margin covers measurement error, valve stroke time and how fast the process can move.

    Surge margin = Q1/Qsurge − 1    (at the same speed)(15)
    Qrecycle = Qcontrol line − Qprocess(16)

    Signs of surge: a loud periodic thump or roar, oscillating flow and discharge pressure, rising suction temperature, axial position and vibration excursions. Operators who have heard it never forget it.

    The surge line moves with gas composition and inlet conditions, so controllers use coordinates (such as reduced head against reduced flow) in which it stays put.

    Five things a surge protection system needs

    A widely cited list from the turbomachinery literature:1

    1. An accurate model of the surge limit over the full range of gas conditions.
    2. A control algorithm that prevents surge without upsetting the process more than necessary.
    3. Instruments fast, accurate and wide-ranging enough.
    4. A recycle valve that handles both large, rapid changes and small, slow ones.
    5. A recycle valve and loop matched to the system volumes: a large volume between the compressor and the check valve can make a single-valve system unable to prevent surge on shutdown.

    Layout rules I check on every P&ID

    • The anti-surge valve must be able to stroke fully open quickly, in less than about 2 seconds.21
    • Take the recycle off downstream of the aftercooler and upstream of the discharge check valve; return it upstream of the suction scrubber.
    • Put the check valve as close to the compressor as the recycle take-off allows. Every cubic metre of gas trapped between the compressor and the check valve must blow back through the machine on a trip.
    • Open the anti-surge valve on every trip. Undersized recycle piping or a slow valve is the usual cause of reverse rotation after a trip.22
    • Size the valve for the whole map, not just the normal point, and check its capacity at the lowest suction pressure.
    • Give each section of a multisection machine its own loop.

    Hot-gas bypass. Some machines add a small, very fast valve from discharge directly to suction, upstream of the cooler, used only on a trip. It reacts faster because the loop volume is small, but it must close quickly or it will overheat the suction.

    3. Surge margin for each case at its own speed, with equation (15). A case that needs recycle at normal conditions is a design error.
    4. Speed range: every case should fall between minimum and maximum continuous speed, with room for heavier and lighter gas than specified.
    5. Discharge temperatures against seal, material and process limits.
    6. Test basis and tolerances. An ASME PTC 10 Type 1 test uses the specified gas at conditions close to site; a Type 2 test uses a substitute gas, often nitrogen or CO₂, at conditions chosen for aerodynamic similarity, with limits on Mach number, Reynolds number and volume ratio.2324 API 617's acceptance tolerances have changed between editions (the 7th edition, for example, required head within 100–105 % of normal and power no more than 107 %), so quote the edition in your purchase order.25

    Moving a map to another gas

    At a given speed, a point on the map keeps its actual inlet flow and its head when the gas changes, as long as the Mach number does not change much. To find what the machine does on a new gas, read Q1 and Hp off the vendor's curve, then use equation (5) with the new M, T1, Z and k to get the new ratio. Example 6 works through it. For large changes in molar mass, ask the vendor to rerun the prediction; the equivalent-speed corrections they use are beyond a hand check.

    Drivers, seals and auxiliaries

    Drivers. Induction or synchronous motors at fixed speed, with a gearbox for most centrifugals; motors on variable-frequency drives for variable speed; steam turbines (API 611 for general purpose, API 612 for special purpose); gas turbines (API 616); and gas engines on field recips. Gears follow API 613 or API 677 and couplings API 671. Size the driver for at least 110 % of the maximum power at any specified condition.17

    Shaft seals. Labyrinths for air and non-hazardous gas; oil-film seals on older machines; and dry gas seals on most modern process centrifugals and screws. A dry gas seal runs on a film of filtered seal gas a few micrometres thick. Tandem seals with an intermediate labyrinth vent the primary leakage to flare and keep the outer seal as a backup, the normal choice for flammable or toxic gas. API 692 (2018) covers dry gas seals and their support systems in four parts: general requirements, seals, support systems, and installation and commissioning.26 Seal gas must be clean and kept above its dew point; a booster may be needed at start-up and during settle-out, when no differential pressure is available.

    Lube and seal oil. API 614 covers lubrication, oil-type shaft-sealing and oil-control systems, but not dry gas seal systems.27 Expect a main and a standby pump with auto-start, coolers, twin filters, a rundown tank or accumulator, and low-pressure alarm and trip.

    Machinery protection. API 670 covers radial shaft vibration, casing vibration, axial position, speed and overspeed, piston rod drop and critical temperatures such as bearing metal and motor windings.28 Where a trip is a safety instrumented function, it also falls under the IEC 61511 lifecycle.29

    Scrubbers, coolers and bottles. Suction scrubbers with mist eliminators, intercoolers, aftercoolers and pulsation bottles are pressure vessels: ASME Section VIII in North America, and in Canada each design also needs a Canadian Registration Number under CSA B51, issued in British Columbia by Technical Safety BC.30

    Starting torque. A compressor restarted at settle-out pressure needs more torque than one started from low pressure. Check that the motor and VFD can start it, or depressure first.

    Depressuring

    A compressor building full of hydrocarbon is usually fitted with automatic blowdown. API 521 bases the rate on fire exposure: reduce the pressure to about 50 % of the vessel's design pressure within roughly 15 minutes, a criterion written for carbon steel walls of about 25 mm and thicker; thinner walls need faster depressuring.52 The logic is that a fire-heated wall loses strength, so halving the pressure halves the stress and buys time before rupture.53

    Two incidents worth reading

    A pressure gauge on a new compressor system was moved onto a longer pipe during troubleshooting and left there. The longer pipe amplified the compressor's vibration until the connection broke and released a large volume of flammable vapour, which did not ignite. A small, temporary change had needed a management-of-change review.51

    An operator opened a high-pressure condensate drain on an air booster with a fast-opening valve. Rapid compression of the air ignited hydrocarbon deposits inside the drain line, the line ruptured and the operator was seriously injured.54 Lubricated air compressors carry fuel, oxygen and heat at the same time; the safeguards in Table 6 exist because of incidents like this one.

    What I bring to a compressor HAZOP: the map with every case on it, the settle-out pressure, the maximum discharge pressure case, the relief valve list, the trip list from the vendor, and the scrubber level and liquid-handling design.

    Worked examples

    Each example uses the equations above with the numbers shown, so you can follow it with a calculator. R = 8.314 kJ/kmol·K; standard conditions are 15 °C and 1.01325 bar.

    Example 1. Standard flow is not what the compressor sees

    Given
    Natural gas, 1.2 million Sm³/d, M = 18.5 kg/kmol. Compressor suction 30 bara, 30 °C, Z1 = 0.93.
    Find
    Molar, mass and actual inlet flows.
    1. Molar volume at standard conditions: 8.314 × 288.15 / 101.325 = 23.64 m³/kmol
    2. Molar and mass flow: 1.2×10⁶ / 23.64 / 24 = 2,115 kmol/h; × 18.5 = 39,120 kg/h = 10.87 kg/s
    3. Actual inlet flow, equation (2): Q₁ = 2,115 × 0.93 × 8.314 × 303.15 / 3,000 = 1,652 m³/h

    50,000 Sm³/h is 1,650 m³/h at the flange, 30 times less. Selection charts, maps and cylinders all work in actual inlet flow.

    Example 2. Head, discharge temperature and power of a centrifugal booster

    Given
    The rated point of Figure 1: M = 20, k = 1.30, Zavg = 0.96, 20 bara and 35 °C to 31.6 bara, 6,000 m³/h actual inlet flow, ηp = 80 %. Mechanical losses 2 %.
    Find
    Polytropic head, discharge temperature, mass flow, power, and the equivalent isentropic efficiency.
    1. Ratio and exponent, equation (6): r = 31.6/20 = 1.580; (n−1)/n = 0.30/(1.30 × 0.80) = 0.2885, n = 1.405
    2. Gas term: Z R T₁/M = 0.96 × (8.314/20) × 308.15 = 122.98 kJ/kg
    3. Head, equation (5): H_p = 122.98/0.2885 × (1.580^0.2885 − 1) = 426.3 × 0.1410 = 60.1 kJ/kg
    4. Discharge temperature, equation (7): T₂ = 308.15 × 1.1410 = 351.6 K = 78.5 °C
    5. Mass flow from equation (1): ρ₁ = 20×10⁵ × 20 / (0.96 × 8,314 × 308.15) = 16.26 kg/m³ ṁ = 16.26 × 6,000/3,600 = 27.10 kg/s
    6. Power, equation (8): P_gas = 27.10 × 60.1/0.80 = 2,037 kW; P_shaft = 2,037 × 1.02 = 2,078 kW
    7. Isentropic efficiency, equation (9), and a cross-check: η_s = (1.580^0.2308 − 1)/(1.580^0.2885 − 1) = 0.1113/0.1410 = 78.9 % H_s = 59.3 kJ/kg; 59.3/0.789 = 75.2 kJ/kg = 60.1/0.80 ✓

    The same machine is "80 % efficient" or "78.9 % efficient" depending on the definition, and both give the same 75.2 kJ/kg of work. Always say which one you mean.

    Example 3. Why plant air is compressed in stages

    Given
    Air (M = 28.96, k = 1.40, Z = 1), 1.0 kg/s from 1.0 bara and 25 °C to 8.0 bara. ηp = 80 % per stage, perfect intercooling to 25 °C, no interstage pressure drop.
    Find
    Discharge temperature and gas power for one, two and three stages.
    1. Constants: (n−1)/n = 0.40/(1.40 × 0.80) = 0.3571; R T₁/M = (8.314/28.96) × 298.15 = 85.60 kJ/kg
    2. One stage, r = 8: T₂ = 298.15 × 8^0.3571 = 626.6 K = 353 °C H = 85.60/0.3571 × 1.1015 = 264.0 kJ/kg; P = 330 kW
    3. Two stages, r = 2.828 each: T₂ = 159 °C; H = 2 × 107.8 = 215.5 kJ/kg; P = 269 kW (−18 %)
    4. Three stages, r = 2.000 each: T₂ = 109 °C; H = 3 × 67.3 = 202.0 kJ/kg; P = 252 kW (−24 %)
    5. Isothermal minimum, equation (3): W = 85.60 × ln 8 = 178 kW

    One stage would deliver air at 353 °C, far above the 150 °C ceiling for oil-lubricated air systems.50 Two stages are still too hot for most designs; three are comfortable. Load the plant-air preset in the calculator to see it.

    Example 4. Capacity and discharge temperature of a hydrogen cylinder

    Given
    Double-acting cylinder: bore 250 mm, stroke 200 mm, rod 60 mm, 375 rpm, clearance 14 %. Hydrogen make-up from 20 to 56 bara (r = 2.8), suction 40 °C, k = 1.41.
    Find
    Displacement, volumetric efficiency, capacity and the ideal discharge temperature.
    1. Areas: A = π × 0.250²/4 = 0.04909 m²; a = π × 0.060²/4 = 0.00283 m²
    2. Displacement, equation (11): PD = (2 × 0.04909 − 0.00283) × 0.200 × 375 × 60 = 429 m³/h
    3. Compressibility from an equation of state: Z_s = 1.011 (20 bara, 40 °C); Z_d = 1.026 (56 bara, ~150 °C); Z_s/Z_d = 0.985
    4. Volumetric efficiency, equation (12): VE = 100 − 2.8 − 14 × (0.985 × 2.8^(1/1.41) − 1) = 100 − 2.8 − 14.6 = 82.6 %
    5. Capacity: 429 × 0.826 = 354 m³/h ρ₁ = 20×10⁵ × 2.016/(1.011 × 8,314 × 313.15) = 1.532 kg/m³ → 543 kg/h
    6. Ideal discharge temperature: T₂ = 313.15 × 2.8^(0.41/1.41) = 313.15 × 1.349 = 422.5 K = 149 °C

    Even the ideal temperature is above the 135 °C API 618 limit for hydrogen-rich gas,2 and the real one is higher. A ratio of about 2.3 per stage (126 °C ideal) is the practical ceiling, which is why a 20 → 160 bara make-up compressor has three stages, not two (Problem 5).

    Example 5. Checking a vendor's normal point

    Given
    The "Normal" column of Table 3: M = 20, k = 1.30, Zavg = 0.96, 20.0 bara and 35 °C, 87,820 kg/h, 30.54 bara, ηp = 79.9 %. The vendor quotes 55.4 kJ/kg, 75.1 °C, 1,690 kW gas power, 9,490 rpm and a surge flow of 3,700 m³/h at that speed.
    Find
    Whether the numbers hang together, and the surge margin.
    1. Inlet flow: ρ₁ = 16.26 kg/m³ (Example 2); 87,820/16.26 = 5,400 m³/h
    2. Head: r = 1.527; (n−1)/n = 0.30/(1.30 × 0.799) = 0.2888 H_p = 122.98/0.2888 × (1.527^0.2888 − 1) = 55.4 kJ/kg ✓
    3. Temperature: T₂ = 308.15 × 1.1300 = 348.2 K = 75.1 °C ✓
    4. Power: 24.39 kg/s × 55.4/0.799 = 1,690 kW ✓; shaft ≈ 1,724 kW
    5. Surge margin, equation (15): 5,400/3,700 − 1 = 46 %, well clear of the 10 % control line
    6. Speed: 9,490/10,000 = 95 %, leaving room for a heavier or lighter gas

    Five minutes with a calculator. When head or power is more than 1–2 % off, ask which Z, k and calculation method the vendor used before discussing efficiency.

    Example 6. A lighter gas on the same machine

    Given
    At 100 % speed and 6,000 m³/h the machine of Figure 1 makes 60 kJ/kg. An upstream change drops the molar mass from 20 to 17 (Z = 0.97, other properties unchanged). The process still needs 31.6 bara.
    Find
    The new discharge pressure, the speed needed to restore it, and what happens at maximum continuous speed (105 %).
    1. Same speed and flow give the same head, 60 kJ/kg. New gas term: 0.97 × (8.314/17) × 308.15 = 146.2 kJ/kg
    2. New ratio, equation (5) solved for r: r = (1 + 60 × 0.2885/146.2)^(1/0.2885) = 1.474 → P₂ = 29.5 bara, 2.1 bar short
    3. Head needed for r = 1.578: 146.2/0.2885 × (1.578^0.2885 − 1) = 71.3 kJ/kg
    4. Speed needed at the same flow, equation (14): √(71.3/60) = 1.09 → 109 %, above the 105 % limit
    5. At 105 % the machine reaches 71.3 kJ/kg only near the left end of its curve: Q ≈ 4,280 m³/h, left of the surge control line at 4,505 m³/h → recycle opens

    A 15 % drop in molar mass turns a comfortable design into a recycle problem. Put every credible composition on the datasheet as a case.1920 Try it with the light-gas case in Figure 1.

    Example 7. How much gas the anti-surge valve recycles

    Given
    Figure 1 machine at 90 % speed. Surge flow at that speed is 0.65 × 6,000 × 0.90 = 3,510 m³/h; the control line is 10 % to the right. The process now takes only 3,000 m³/h (at suction conditions).
    Find
    Recycle flow and the power spent on it.
    1. Control line: 1.10 × 3,510 = 3,861 m³/h
    2. Recycle, equation (16): 3,861 − 3,000 = 861 m³/h, 22 % of the compressor's flow
    3. Operating point on the control line: H = 52.3 kJ/kg, η_p = 77.6 %, ṁ = 16.26 × 3,861/3,600 = 17.44 kg/s
    4. Power: 17.44 × 52.3/0.776 = 1,175 kW gas power of which 22 % ≈ 262 kW compresses recycled gas

    Recycle protects the machine, but it is a heater fed with electricity. If the plant will run here for months, rerate the impellers or run fewer machines in parallel.

    Example 8. Settle-out pressure after a trip

    Given
    The compressor trips and the discharge check valve closes. Suction side (scrubber, piping, casing) 25 m³ at 20 bara and 35 °C; discharge side up to the check valve (piping, aftercooler) 15 m³ at 31.6 bara and 40 °C. The gas settles at 40 °C; ignore the change in Z.
    Find
    Settle-out pressure.
    1. Moles are proportional to PV/T: suction 20 × 25/308.15 = 1.623; discharge 31.6 × 15/313.15 = 1.514
    2. Settle-out: P_so = (1.623 + 1.514) × 313.15/40 = 24.6 bara

    The suction scrubber normally sees 20 bara, but after every trip it sees 24.6. Its design pressure must cover settle-out with margin, or its relief valve must be sized for it. Settle-out also sets the restart conditions and the starting point of blowdown calculations.49 If the check valve leaks, the downstream system joins the balance and the pressure climbs further.

    Example 9. CO₂ compression for carbon capture

    Given
    100 t/h of CO₂ from a capture plant at 1.2 bara and 40 °C, compressed to 30 bara in four intercooled stages (back to 40 °C), ηp = 80 %, k = 1.29, no interstage pressure drop.
    Find
    Stage ratio, head, discharge temperature and power; then compare with a rigorous real-gas calculation.
    1. Ratio and exponent: r_stage = (30/1.2)^(1/4) = 2.236; (n−1)/n = 0.29/(1.29 × 0.80) = 0.2810
    2. Average Z per stage from an equation of state: 0.995, 0.988, 0.974, 0.940
    3. Heads, equation (5): 53.1 + 52.8 + 52.0 + 50.2 = 208.1 kJ/kg T₂ = 313.15 × 2.236^0.2810 = 119.5 °C each stage
    4. Power: 27.78 kg/s × 208.1/0.80 = 7.23 MW gas power, about 74 kWh per tonne at the shaft
    5. Rigorous check, integrating dh = v dP/ηp with the Span–Wagner equation of state: 207.1 kJ/kg (0.5 % lower); T₂ = 114–115 °C

    The average-Z method is good to half a percent here and slightly conservative on temperature. Beyond about 74 bar and 31 °C CO₂ is supercritical; pipelines carry it in the dense phase, typically above 86 bar,55 and some designs finish with a pump rather than more compressor stages.56 Integrally geared machines suit the duty because each stage can run at its own speed and be intercooled.4

    4,510 − 3,800 = 710 m³/h (16 % of the compressor's flow)

    Problem 11. Settle-out of a two-stage machine

    After a trip the recycle valves open and three volumes equalize: suction 12 m³ at 8 bara and 30 °C, interstage 9 m³ at 22 bara and 40 °C, and discharge 6 m³ at 60 bara and 45 °C. The gas settles at 40 °C; ignore Z. Find the settle-out pressure.

    bara
    Σ PV/T = 8×12/303.15 + 22×9/313.15 + 60×6/318.15 = 0.3167 + 0.6323 + 1.1315 = 2.0805 P_so = 2.0805 × 313.15/27 = 24.1 bara

    Three times the normal suction pressure. The first-stage scrubber is designed for this, not for 8 bara.

    Problem 12. Driver rating

    Across all datasheet cases, including 105 % speed and the heaviest gas, the highest compressor shaft power is 3,120 kW. What minimum driver rating does API 617 ask for?

    kW
    1.10 × 3,120 = 3,432 kW → next motor size up

    Add gear and coupling losses if they sit between the compressor and the driver.

    Problem 13. What the intercooler is worth

    Air goes from 1 to 9 bara at 25 °C (ηp = 80 %, k = 1.40). What percentage of gas power do two equal intercooled stages save over one stage?

    %
    One stage: H = 239.7 × (9^0.3571 − 1) = 285.6 kJ/kg Two stages: H = 2 × 239.7 × (3^0.3571 − 1) = 230.3 kJ/kg Saving = 1 − 230.3/285.6 = 19.4 %

    Problem 14. Gas rod load

    A double-acting cylinder has a 250 mm bore and a 63.5 mm rod. Suction is 15 bara and discharge 45 bara. Using gauge pressures, find the gas rod load in compression (head end compressing). Then find the load in tension.

    kN
    A = 0.04909 m²; a = 0.00317 m²; P_d = 44 barg; P_s = 14 barg Compression: 44×10⁵ × 0.04909 − 14×10⁵ × 0.04592 = 216.0 − 64.3 = 151.7 kN Tension: 44×10⁵ × 0.04592 − 14×10⁵ × 0.04909 = 202.1 − 68.7 = 133.3 kN

    Compare with the frame's allowable loads at the relief-valve set pressure, not just at normal discharge pressure.

    Problem 15. Spot the error

    A revised proposal keeps every number in the low-molar-mass column of Table 3 but lists the gas power as 1,650 kW. Calculate the gas power from the other numbers. Is the revision credible?

    kW
    84,450/3,600 × 62.7/0.799 = 23.46 × 78.5 = 1,841 kW

    1,650 kW would need either 89 % polytropic efficiency or a head of 56 kJ/kg. One of the numbers is wrong; ask before you evaluate the bid.

    Problem 16. The hot afternoon

    On a summer afternoon the suction temperature of the Figure 1 machine rises from 35 to 50 °C at fixed speed while the process holds 31.6 bara. Explain what happens on the map.

    For the same ratio the head rises with T1: from 60.1 to 63.1 kJ/kg (equation 5). At fixed speed the machine can only make more head at lower flow, so the operating point slides left along its speed line toward surge, and the inlet density falls by 5 %, so mass flow drops further. Remedies: raise the speed, restore suction cooling, or use inlet guide vanes where fitted. This is why many plants see their compressor limit in summer and their driver limit in winter.

    Problem 17. Recycle and check valve placement

    Where do you take off the anti-surge recycle, where does it return, and where does the discharge check valve go? Give reasons.

    Take the recycle off downstream of the aftercooler, so recycled gas does not heat the suction; return it upstream of the suction scrubber, so any condensate is knocked out. Place the check valve just downstream of the recycle take-off and as close to the compressor as that allows, to minimise the volume that must flow back through the machine on a trip. The valve must open fully in about 2 seconds and be sized for the whole map, and the trip logic must open it.

    Problem 18. Starting against a closed valve

    An operator wants to start a reciprocating compressor with its discharge block valve closed "to load it gently". What happens, and what should the procedure be?

    A positive displacement machine keeps pushing gas into the trapped volume, and the pressure rises every stroke until the relief valve lifts or something fails: a gasket, a cylinder head, the rod or the crosshead. If the relief valve sits downstream of the closed block valve, nothing protects the cylinder. Start unloaded instead: suction unloaders held open or the start-up bypass open, discharge valve open, then load in steps. The relief valve belongs between the cylinder and the first block valve, and discharge block valves should be interlocked or locked open in service.

    What goes on the datasheet

    The process engineer owns the process side of the compressor datasheet, and the vendor can only design for the cases written on it. I fill in every line below before a vendor sees it.

    • Gas composition in mol % for every case: normal, rated, minimum, start-up, regeneration and each credible alternative gas
    • Trace components: H₂S, CO₂, water, mercury, oxygen, chlorides, ammonia, anything that polymerizes
    • Properties: M, k, and Z at suction and discharge, with the equation of state used
    • Flows: mass flow for each case, with the basis of any margin; actual inlet flow as a check
    • Suction: pressure and temperature, minimum, normal and maximum; humidity for air
    • Discharge pressure for each case; maximum allowable downstream pressure
    • Sidestreams in or out for multisection machines, with their pressures and flows
    • Settle-out pressure and the volumes it was calculated from
    • Liquids and solids: scrubber performance, droplet sizes, fouling tendency
    • Temperature limits: process, seal and material
    • Control and turndown: method, range, parallel operation, anti-surge requirements
    • Hazards: flammability, toxicity, area classification; seal and distance-piece requirements
    • Materials: sour service, hydrogen partial pressure, corrosion allowances
    • Utilities: cooling water supply and return temperatures, seal gas source, nitrogen, power
    • Driver: type, speed range, voltage, VFD, starting conditions
    • Site: elevation, ambient range, winterization, noise limits
    • Testing: PTC 10 Type 1 or 2, mechanical run test, string test, witness points
    • Standards: API 617, 618, 619 or 672, with edition and company supplements; API 614, 670 and 692 for auxiliaries
