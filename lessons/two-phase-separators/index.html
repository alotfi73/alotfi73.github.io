Two-phase gas–liquid separators

Flash drums, knockout drums and scrubbers: how to choose the type, size a vertical or horizontal vessel, pick the internals, read mist-eliminator and packaged-separator vendor data, apply ASME, API 12J and API 521, and keep liquid out of places it should never reach. A separator is the plainest-looking vessel on a P&ID and one of the most consequential. When it fails, liquid ends up in a compressor, a fuel-gas header or a flare, and gas ends up in a vessel that was never designed for it.

01

What a separator does

A two-phase separator takes a stream that is part gas and part liquid and sends each phase out of its own nozzle, with as little of the other phase as the downstream equipment can tolerate. Whatever the orientation, it does this in four zones, which the GPSA Engineering Data Book labels A to D.1

  1. A, the inlet or primary section. An inlet device absorbs the momentum of the feed and drops out most of the liquid as bulk liquid. A good inlet device also spreads the gas evenly across the vessel. A bad one shatters liquid into fine mist that nothing downstream can catch.
  2. B, the gravity settling section. The gas slows down and droplets fall out under gravity. It is usually sized so that droplets of about 100–150 µm and larger settle before the gas reaches the mist eliminator.712
  3. C, the mist extraction section. A mesh pad, vane pack or bank of cyclones catches the fine droplets, down to around 10 µm or smaller, by impaction and coalescence.68
  4. D, the liquid collection section. The liquid gives up its entrained gas bubbles, and the volume between the level settings gives the control system and the operators time to react.

Most sizing arguments are about which of these zones runs out of room first. In a scrubber with a little condensate, it is the gas zones B and C. In a flash drum taking a large liquid flow, it is zone D.

Flash drum, knockout drum, scrubber. The names overlap. A flash drum receives a liquid that partly vaporizes on entry; a knockout drum or scrubber removes a small amount of liquid from a mostly gas stream. The sizing method is the same.

Levels, holdup and surge

The liquid zone is defined by its level settings. From the bottom up they are usually the low-low trip (LALL), the low alarm (LAL), the normal level (NLL), the high alarm (LAH) and the high-high trip (LAHH). Svrcek and Monnery give the definitions most sizing procedures use:2

  • Holdup is the time for the level to fall from normal to the low-low level at the normal outlet flow with no feed. It protects whatever the liquid goes to next, such as a pump, a column or a heater.
  • Surge is the time for the level to rise from normal to the high-high level at the normal feed rate with the outlet closed. It absorbs slugs and upsets. Without a better basis, Svrcek and Monnery take surge as half the holdup time.

Some companies measure surge between the low and high levels instead. Which definition you use matters less than writing it on the data sheet so the vendor sizes the same volume you did.

Tag names vary. API RP 14C calls the shutdown sensors LSH and LSL; many P&IDs use LAHH and LALL, or HHLL and LLLL. Check the legend sheet before comparing two designs.

02

Types and where they are used

Orientation

Vertical separators are the usual choice when the gas-to-liquid ratio is high or the total gas flow is modest, as in scrubbers and knockout drums.1 They need less plot space, which matters offshore, and they make solids easier to remove from the bottom head.15 A given change in liquid volume also moves the level further, which makes level control and trips more responsive. Their weakness is that droplets must fall against the rising gas, so the full cross-section is limited by the settling velocity.

Horizontal separators win when there is a lot of liquid, when the liquid foams or carries dissolved gas that needs a large surface to escape, when slugs arrive, or when a second liquid phase must also settle. Droplets fall across the gas flow rather than against it, so for the same flow area a horizontal drum can run at a higher gas velocity than a vertical one.33 The cost is plot space and a level that moves less for a given volume change.

Spherical separators are compact and strong, but they have little surge volume and are hard to fit with internals. They are rare in new plants and are outside the scope of API RP 12J.4

A quick screen. Run Figure 1 for your duty. If the height-to-diameter ratio comes out above about 5 even after widening, or the liquid holdup is several cubic metres, price a horizontal drum as well.

Services you will meet

Flash drum

A liquid enters below its bubble point downstream of a let-down valve or a heater and partly vaporizes. Examples are amine and glycol flash tanks, hot and cold high-pressure separators in hydrotreaters, and stage separators in gas plants.

Vertical or horizontal, usually with a mesh pad. The feed nozzle sees flashing two-phase flow.

Knockout drum

Removes free liquid and condensate from a gas: fuel-gas knockout drums, upstream of burners and gas turbines, ahead of meters and analysers.

Vertical, with a mesh pad or vane pack.

Compressor suction scrubber

The last line of defence for a compressor. Liquid slugs break valves in reciprocating machines and erode impellers in centrifugal ones, so the scrubber has a conservative K and a high-high level trip that stops the compressor.

Vertical, mesh pad, vane pack or cyclones. GPSA multiplies K by 0.7–0.8 for this service.1

Inlet, production and test separators

Separate well fluids into gas and liquid in oil and gas fields. Test separators measure the rates from one well at a time.

Usually horizontal. API RP 12J and API Spec 12J cover them.45

Reflux drum or overhead accumulator

Collects condensed distillation overhead, supplies reflux and product, and vents non-condensables. Often carries a water boot.

Horizontal; the holdup is usually set on the reflux flow.28

Flare knockout drum

Removes liquid from relief and blowdown streams so the flare does not rain burning liquid. Sized for droplets of 300–600 µm and 20–30 minutes of holdup for the largest liquid release.24

Usually horizontal, no internals. Covered by API 521, not API RP 12J.416

Slug catcher

Receives liquid slugs at the end of a multiphase pipeline. Above about 35 bar, a manifold of large pipes (a finger-type slug catcher) is often cheaper than one thick-walled vessel.34

Vessel type or finger type; sized on the largest expected slug volume.

Steam drum and evaporator separator

Separates steam from boiler water, or vapour from a boiling liquor. Steam purity sets the design, so high-efficiency mesh is common.

Horizontal steam drums; vertical evaporator bodies.8

Filter separator and coalescer

Removes submicron aerosols that a mesh pad cannot, ahead of membranes, amine contactors, turboexpanders and gas turbines.

Filter elements followed by a vane section. Outside API RP 12J.4

Compact and inline cyclonic separators

Use swirl instead of gravity to save weight and space, mostly offshore and in debottlenecks.

Proprietary designs, sized by the vendor. Outside API RP 12J.4

03

The flash: what arrives at the separator

A separator does not decide how much gas and liquid it receives. Thermodynamics does, and the separator only decides how cleanly they part. In design the vapour and liquid rates, compositions and densities come from a flash calculation, usually in a simulator. It is still worth being able to do one by hand, because a hand flash is how you catch a wrong thermodynamic package, a missing component or a feed entered on the wrong basis.

For an isothermal flash of F kmol/h of feed with mole fractions zi into V kmol/h of vapour and L of liquid, with equilibrium ratios Ki = yi/xi, the vapour fraction β = V/F is the root of the Rachford–Rice equation:

Σ zi(Ki − 1) / [1 + β(Ki − 1)] = 0(1)
xi = zi / [1 + β(Ki − 1)]    yi = Ki xi(2)

The left side of (1) falls steadily as β goes from 0 to 1. If it is positive at β = 1, the feed is all vapour; if it is negative at β = 0, it is all liquid. Otherwise there is exactly one root between them, which a few bisection steps will find.

For a quick estimate of K at low to moderate pressure, the Wilson correlation uses only critical properties and the acentric factor:

Ki ≈ (pc,i/p) exp[5.373 (1 + ωi)(1 − Tc,i/T)](3)

A drum downstream of a let-down valve is an adiabatic flash: the feed enthalpy is conserved, so the drum temperature is found by adjusting T until the vapour and liquid enthalpies, weighted by β, equal the feed enthalpy. That temperature drop is how separators downstream of choke and let-down valves end up cold enough to form hydrates or to need impact-tested steel.

Example 1 in section 12 runs a six-component flash by hand. Its vapour and liquid rates feed Examples 2 to 8.

Use Wilson for checking, not design. It ignores composition and is poor near the critical point. A cubic equation of state such as Peng–Robinson is the normal basis for hydrocarbon separators.

Size for every case. Summer and winter, start-of-life and end-of-life, turndown and upset. The case that sets the diameter is often not the one that sets the height.

04

How droplets settle

A droplet of diameter d in a rising gas feels its weight less buoyancy pulling it down, and drag pushing it up. It stops accelerating when they balance, at its terminal velocity:

(π d³/6)(ρL − ρG) g = CD (π d²/4) ρG vt²/2(4)
vt = √[ 4 g d (ρL − ρG) / (3 CD ρG) ](5)

The drag coefficient CD depends on the droplet Reynolds number Re = ρG vt d / μG, so (5) is solved by iteration. A smooth-sphere correlation that covers the whole range up to Re ≈ 2 × 10⁵ is Turton and Levenspiel’s:22

CD = (24/Re)(1 + 0.173 Re0.657) + 0.413 / (1 + 16,300 Re−1.09)(6)

Two limits are useful. At Re below about 1, CD = 24/Re and (5) becomes Stokes’ law, vt = g d² (ρL − ρG) / (18 μG): velocity rises with the square of the diameter. At Re above about 1,000, CD is close to 0.44 and velocity rises only with the square root of the diameter. Most droplets in process separators fall between the two.

Rearranging (5) shows where the Souders–Brown equation comes from:

vt = K √[(ρL − ρG)/ρG]    with    K = √[ 4 g d / (3 CD) ](7)

K has units of velocity and carries the droplet size and the drag. For a separator without a mist eliminator, K is a statement about which droplet size you are willing to let through. For a separator with a mist eliminator, K is an empirical limit on the device, set by re-entrainment and flooding, and droplet physics no longer sets it directly.

Design droplet sizes. About 100–150 µm for a gravity section ahead of a mist eliminator, 300–600 µm for flare knockout drums, and 10 µm and below for the mist eliminator itself.7246

Droplets are not spheres of one size. Real inlet streams carry a distribution of sizes set mainly by the inlet piping and inlet device. That is why the inlet momentum limits in section 08 matter as much as K.

Two numbers from Figure 3 are worth remembering. At the hydrocarbon conditions, the GPSA K for a vertical drum without a mist eliminator (half of 0.093, or 0.047 m/s) corresponds to a droplet of about 180 µm. The mesh-pad K of 0.093 m/s corresponds to about 390 µm. So a vertical drum running at its mesh-pad limit carries droplets of several hundred microns up to the pad, and the pad, not gravity, does most of the work. That is why the mist eliminator is sized so carefully, and why it is the first thing to check when a drum carries over.

05

The K-factor and the Souders–Brown equation

The Souders–Brown equation gives the maximum allowable gas velocity, and with it the minimum flow area:

Vmax = K √[(ρL − ρG)/ρG]    Amin = QG / Vmax(8)

QG is the actual gas flow at separator conditions (m³/s), not a standard flow. A is the full cross-section of a vertical vessel, or the face area of the mist eliminator.

The equation is simple; choosing K is the engineering. The values below are the starting points used in industry. They are for clean, non-foaming systems and for the mist eliminators described, and they assume a reasonable inlet device and an even gas distribution.

Units. K in m/s here; 1 ft/s = 0.3048 m/s, so the classic 0.35 ft/s is 0.107 m/s.

Some companies call the term √(ρG/(ρL−ρG)) × V the “load factor”. It is the same K.

Why K falls with pressure

The Souders–Brown equation already lowers the velocity as gas density rises, so the extra derating in Table 1 can look like double counting. It is not. As pressure rises the gas-to-liquid density ratio rises and the surface tension falls, so droplets are smaller, break up more easily and are re-entrained from wet surfaces at lower velocities. Campbell attributes the decline of mesh pad K with pressure mainly to these two effects.7

When to lower K further

  • High liquid load to the mist eliminator. Above about 0.75 US gpm/ft² (1.8 m³/m²·h) for a standard mesh pad, Campbell reduces K by 10 % for each additional 1 gpm/ft² (2.4 m³/m²·h).7
  • Foaming or low surface tension: glycol, amine, some crudes and condensates near their critical point.
  • Fouling, wax or solids: use a lower K with an open mesh, or a vane pack, and plan for cleaning.
  • Slugs and surges: vendors apply a derating factor for these exceptional conditions.8

The opposite mistake is just as real. A mesh pad needs enough velocity for droplets to hit the wires. Below roughly a quarter of its design velocity it loses efficiency, and droplets pass straight through.8 A scrubber sized for a future flow can therefore carry over today.

Which K is the vendor quoting? A vane K at the vane face can be 0.45 m/s while the same vessel’s cross-section runs at 0.15 m/s. Always ask what area a K refers to.

Sizing a vertical separator

This is the procedure behind Figure 1. It follows Svrcek and Monnery’s method with GPSA K values, which is still the most common hand method in process design offices.21

Step 1. Gas and liquid at separator conditions

Convert every flow to actual volume at the separator’s pressure and temperature. For the gas,

ρG = p M / (Z R T)    QG = ṁG / ρG(9)

p in Pa absolute, M in kg/kmol, R = 8,314 J/(kmol·K), T in K. Take Z from the same equation of state as the flash.

Step 2. Diameter from the gas

Pick K from Tables 1 and 2, derate it for pressure and service, and find Vmax from equation (8). The mist-eliminator diameter follows, and the vessel is made slightly larger to leave room for the support ring:

DME = √(4 QG / (π Vmax))    D ≥ DME + 75 to 150 mm(10)

Svrcek and Monnery add 3–6 in and round up to the next 6 in (about 150 mm).3 Without a mist eliminator the whole cross-section is the gravity section and no ring allowance is needed.

Step 3. Liquid heights from holdup and surge

hH = tH QL / A    hS = tS QL / A(11)

Holdup and surge times are set by what the liquid goes to and how good the control is. Table 3 gives the usual starting points.

Volumes in the heads. Hand methods usually ignore the liquid in the bottom head, which gives a little extra margin. Include it when you are rating an existing vessel and every minute counts.

Vertical two-phase separators usually end up with a height-to-diameter ratio of about 3 to 5.12 If H/D is well above that, the liquid is controlling: increase the diameter, which shortens the liquid column, and repeat. If H/D is below about 2, the fixed spacings are controlling and the vessel is about as small as it can be.

Widening a vessel for liquid has a side effect that catches people out. The gas velocity in a full-diameter mesh pad falls, sometimes below its turndown limit. The fix is to keep the pad sized for the gas: either a full-diameter pad with an annular blanking plate, or a smaller pad in a box around the gas outlet.12 Figure 1 does this automatically when the velocity in a full-diameter device falls below about 25 % of design for mesh (30 % for cyclones, 40 % for vanes). Example 3 works through it.

Step 6. Nozzles, then mechanical design

Size the nozzles from section 08, check the inlet nozzle against the height you allowed for it, then pass the vessel to mechanical design (section 10).

Why 0.5 D? The gas leaving the inlet device needs a vertical distance to spread across the vessel before it reaches the mist eliminator. A wide vessel needs more height to even out the flow.

Don’t hide the trip levels. Show LALL, LAL, NLL, LAH and LAHH as heights above the bottom tangent on the data sheet, with the times they represent.

07

Sizing a horizontal separator

In a horizontal drum the gas flows along the vessel above the liquid, and a droplet released at the top must fall through the gas space before the gas carries it to the outlet. The cross-section is shared, so every calculation needs the area of a circular segment. For a liquid depth h in a drum of diameter D, the fraction of the cross-section below the liquid surface is

f(h/D) = (θ − sin θ) / (2π)    with    θ = 2 arccos(1 − 2h/D)(13)

θ in radians. At h/D = 0.5, f = 0.5; at h/D = 0.25, f = 0.196. The gas area above a level is AT[1 − f(h/D)].

The procedure

Svrcek and Monnery’s method, in SI units:2

  1. Find the settling velocity with a no-mist-eliminator K (half the GPSA value), and use 75 % of it, UV = 0.75 UT, for a conservative design.
  2. Calculate the holdup and surge volumes, VH = tHQL and VS = tSQL.
  3. Guess L/D and get a first diameter, assuming holdup and surge fill about 60 % of the drum:
    D = [ 4(VH + VS) / (0.6 π (L/D)) ]1/3(14)
  4. Set the low-low level. Svrcek and Monnery use 0.5 D(ft) + 7 in, about 180 mm + 0.042 D(mm); many companies use a flat 300 mm minimum.
  5. Set the gas space above the high-high level to at least the larger of 0.2 D and 0.3 m, or 0.6 m with a mist eliminator.
  6. Find the length for the liquid, and the length for the droplets:
    Lliq = (VH + VS) / (AT − AV − ALLL)    Lgas = (QG/AV) × (hV/UV)(15)
  7. Take the larger length. Repeat with other diameters until L/D is between 1.5 and 6, and choose the lightest vessel that meets both. Svrcek and Monnery narrow the range by pressure: 1.5–3 up to 250 psig (about 17 barg), 3–4 from 250 to 500 psig (17–34 barg) and 4–6 above that.

Typical horizontal two-phase separators end up with L/D of 3 to 5.12 Low-pressure drums often end up shorter and fatter, because their walls are thin and the cost follows the shell area, not the wall thickness.

Two more checks are worth making. The gas velocity over the liquid must stay low enough not to whip liquid off the surface; NORSOK’s typical maximum, 0.137 (L/6)0.58 √[(ρL − ρG)/ρG], is a useful screen.14 And the effective length available to the droplets is shorter than the shell: Campbell uses Le = L − D to allow for the inlet and outlet zones.6

Effective length. The distance from the inlet device to the mist eliminator or gas outlet, not the tangent-to-tangent length. A cyclonic inlet or a perforated baffle makes more of the shell useful.

Boots. When a small amount of water must also be removed from a hydrocarbon liquid, a boot under the drum keeps the interface level controllable without lengthening the drum.

Nozzles and internals

A correctly sized vessel with the wrong inlet will carry over. Excess momentum in the feed breaks liquid into fine mist that the mist eliminator cannot catch, and excess momentum in the gas outlet sucks gas through one part of the mist eliminator and leaves the rest idle.37 Nozzles are therefore sized on momentum, ρv², rather than on velocity alone.

Inlet devices

  • None, or an open pipe end. Cheap, and prone to short-circuiting and jetting liquid onto the opposite wall.12
  • Deflector or diverter plate. A flat or dished plate in front of the nozzle. It stops the jet but tends to make small droplets and foam.12
  • Half-open pipe. A pipe stub with its lower half cut away, pointing the flow downward. A simple default for vertical drums with modest momentum.
  • Vane inlet (Schoepentoeter and similar). Rows of curved vanes slice the feed sideways and spread the gas. It is the normal choice at moderate to high momentum for feeds that are mostly gas, and it copes with slugs.8
  • Inlet cyclones. Use the feed momentum to spin out liquid, break foam and help coalescence, at momentums around ten times those of pipe inlets.12

Give the inlet a straight run of about 5–10 pipe diameters with no pockets, so slugs and stratified flow arrive as evenly as possible.38

ρv² is a pressure. 1 kg/(m·s²) = 1 Pa. It is twice the dynamic pressure, which is why vendors sometimes quote “dynamic pressure” limits half the size of a company’s ρv² limit. Check which one a figure is.

  • Vortex breaker on every liquid outlet, usually crossed plates, to stop gas being drawn into the pump suction.26

09

Vendor data and how to read it

Most separator internals are bought, and most packaged separators are selected from a vendor’s range. Vendor data is good data, but it is quoted under the vendor’s conditions, which are rarely yours. Reading it well means finding the basis first and the number second.

Mesh pad styles: four names for the same pad

K-values: read the conditions

Vendor K-values are usually for air and water at ambient conditions: AMACS quotes 0.35 ft/s (0.107 m/s) for vertical flow and 0.42 ft/s for horizontal flow on that basis.9 Sulzer’s standard KnitMesh is quoted at K up to 0.107 m/s, its V-MISTER at up to 0.13 m/s and its 9797 design at up to 0.12 m/s.8 None of these numbers applies directly at 70 bar with a condensate. Derate for pressure (Table 1), liquid load and foaming, and ask the vendor to rate the device at your conditions and your liquid load.

Density is not efficiency. A denser pad has more wire to hit, but it also floods earlier. The fine-wire styles get their efficiency from thinner wire, not just more of it.

Grade efficiency curves

A grade efficiency curve gives the fraction of droplets of each size that the device removes, at one velocity, one pressure and one fluid pair. Read three numbers from it: the size removed at 50 %, at 90 % and at 99 %. Then check four things before you use them:

  1. The test fluid and pressure. Figure 5 shows why: the same pad loses most of its fine-droplet efficiency at high pressure, because the gas is denser, the droplets slow less and the velocity through the pad is far lower.
  2. The velocity. Efficiency for small droplets falls as velocity falls. At turndown the pad you bought may be a coarse filter. Move the velocity slider to 25 % to see it.
  3. The liquid load. Curves are usually for light loads. Above the load limit, capacity falls (section 05).
  4. The droplet size reaching the device. A curve that removes everything above 10 µm is no use if the inlet device is making 5 µm mist.

Pressure drop curves

Pressure drop rises slowly with velocity until the device starts to load with liquid, then rises steeply near flooding. A clean, dry mesh pad is typically under 2.5 mbar, vane packs 1–10 mbar and cyclone decks up to about 30 mbar.8 Two practical uses: a rising ΔP in operation means fouling or flooding, and the device ΔP sets how far liquid rises in its drain pipe, which in turn sets how far below the low-low level the drain must be sealed.

Mechanical design and standards

Process design hands mechanical design a diameter, a height, nozzles, internals, a design pressure and temperature, a corrosion allowance and a material. Mechanical design returns wall thicknesses and a weight, and sometimes a reason to change the diameter. It is worth knowing the shell formulas well enough to check them.

Design pressure and temperature

Design pressure is set above the maximum operating pressure with enough margin for the relief valve to sit above normal fluctuations without simmering; Svrcek and Monnery’s example uses 110 % of operating pressure;2 a common company rule is the greater of 110 % and operating plus 1.7 bar (25 psi), which governs on low-pressure drums. Design temperature covers the highest operating temperature plus a margin, and the minimum design metal temperature (MDMT) covers the coldest case, including auto-refrigeration during depressuring.

Shell and head thickness to ASME VIII Division 1

For internal pressure P, inside radius R and diameter D in the corroded condition, allowable stress S and joint efficiency E, the long-standing Division 1 formulas are:17

Cylindrical shell, hoop stress (UG-27(c)(1)):   t = P R / (S E − 0.6 P)(16)
Cylindrical shell, longitudinal stress (UG-27(c)(2)):   t = P R / (2 S E + 0.4 P)(17)
2:1 ellipsoidal head (UG-32(d)):   t = P D / (2 S E − 0.2 P)    Hemispherical head:   t = P R / (2 S E − 0.2 P)(18)

Valid for the hoop formula when P ≤ 0.385 S E and t ≤ R/2. Add the corrosion allowance and the plate under-tolerance, and allow for thinning when heads are formed.

Rearranged, the same equations give the maximum allowable working pressure (MAWP) of the plate actually chosen, P = S E t / (R + 0.6 t) for the shell. The standard hydrostatic test pressure is 1.3 × MAWP × the ratio of allowable stress at test temperature to that at design temperature.17

The most common carbon-steel plate for separators is SA-516 Grade 70, with an allowable stress of 20,000 psi (138 MPa) at moderate temperatures.42 Check ASME Section II Part D at your design temperature, and the impact-test rules at your MDMT.

Fire

API 521 estimates the heat absorbed by the wetted wall of a vessel in a pool fire as

Q = 43,200 F Aw0.82   W, with adequate drainage and prompt firefighting(19)
Q = 70,900 F Aw0.82   W, without them(20)

Aw is the wetted area in m², counted only up to 7.6 m (25 ft) above grade or the fire source; F is the environment factor, 1.0 for a bare vessel.1640

The relief rate is the heat input divided by the latent heat at relieving conditions, W = Q/λ. Near the critical point, where the latent heat is small, API 521 suggests a minimum of 115 kJ/kg as an approximation.40 The gas-filled upper part of a vessel has no liquid to cool it; its wall can lose strength in a fire before the relief valve lifts, which is why depressuring systems exist alongside relief valves.

Gas blowby

If an upstream separator loses its liquid level and its level control valve is open, gas at upstream pressure flows through a valve sized for liquid into a downstream vessel. When the upstream pressure exceeds the downstream vessel’s MAWP, this is often the largest relief case on the downstream drum. API 521 does not allow credit for a favourable control response, so the valve is assumed to stay where the failure left it, and the bypass valve may have to be assumed open as well.3141 The gas flow through the valve follows the control-valve sizing equation of IEC 60534-2-1:21

W = N6 Cv Y √(x p1 ρ1),   Y = 1 − x/(3 Fγ xT),   x ≤ Fγ xT,   Fγ = k/1.40(21)

W in kg/h with N6 = 27.3 for p1 in bar absolute and ρ1 in kg/m³; x = Δp/p1; xT is the valve’s pressure-differential ratio factor. When x reaches FγxT the flow is choked and Y = 2/3.

The remedies are to size the downstream relief valve for the blowby flow, to limit the valve trim or add a restriction orifice so the blowby flow is smaller without affecting normal liquid flow, or to provide an independent low-low level trip, which some companies accept as a high-integrity protection system in place of relief capacity.41

Liquid carryover

Liquid reaching a compressor, a turbine fuel system or a flare can destroy equipment or start a fire. The defences are a conservative K for the service, an inlet device matched to the inlet momentum, a mist eliminator that works across the whole flow range, and an independent high-high level trip with enough surge volume between LAH and LAHH for the trip to act before liquid reaches the inlet nozzle.

Other hazards

  • Low temperature. Depressuring a vessel, or flashing liquid across its drain valve, can chill steel below its MDMT. Check auto-refrigeration cases and choose impact-tested steel where needed.
  • Hydrates and ice in drains, level bridles and instrument connections: heat tracing, insulation, methanol or glycol injection, and drains that can be cleared safely.
  • Sour service. H₂S is toxic and causes sulfide stress cracking: select materials to NACE MR0175/ISO 15156. Iron sulfide deposits can be pyrophoric when a vessel is opened, so keep them wet and purge with nitrogen.
  • Erosion from sand at the inlet nozzle and the opposite wall: wear plates, inlet devices, and velocities below the erosional limit.
  • Foaming that carries liquid into the gas outlet: a lower K, antifoam, or a cyclonic inlet.
  • Draining to atmosphere. Never drain a pressurized separator to an open drain through a single valve; liquid flashes, the valve can freeze open, and gas follows the liquid.
  • Vessel entry. Separators are confined spaces with residual hydrocarbons, H₂S and nitrogen. Isolation, purging, gas testing and an entry permit come before anyone goes through the manway.

Questions a HAZOP will ask

  • What is the highest pressure any upstream source can impose, and can the vessel see it?
  • What is the blowby flow if the upstream level valve and its bypass fail open?
  • How long between LAH and LAHH, and between LAHH and the inlet nozzle, at the maximum liquid rate?
  • Are the trip transmitters independent of the control transmitter, and tested?
  • What is the coldest the steel can get during depressuring?
  • Where does the liquid go when the drain is opened, and what does it do when it flashes?

The 7.6 m rule. Only the wetted area within 7.6 m of the base of a pool fire is counted, because experience has shown that sizing on the average heat input up to that height is enough.40

Blowby is two-phase first. The upstream vessel’s liquid arrives before its gas. The downstream relief valve can see liquid, then two-phase flow, then gas.31

Example 6. Shell and head thickness to ASME VIII Division 1

Given
The Example 3 vessel, 1,350 mm inside diameter, maximum operating pressure 39.0 barg. Design pressure 110 % of operating. SA-516-70, S = 138 MPa at design temperature; full radiography, E = 1.0; corrosion allowance 3 mm; 2:1 ellipsoidal heads.
Find
Shell and head thickness, the MAWP of the chosen shell plate, and the hydrotest pressure.
  1. Design pressure: P = 1.1 × 39.0 = 42.9 barg = 4.29 MPa
  2. Shell, equation (16), radius in the corroded condition: R = 675 + 3 = 678 mm t = 4.29 × 678 / (138 × 1.0 − 0.6 × 4.29) = 2,909 / 135.4 = 21.5 mm plus corrosion allowance: 24.5 mm → order 26 mm plate checks: P = 4.29 ≤ 0.385 SE = 53.1 MPa; t ≤ R/2
  3. Head, equation (18): t = 4.29 × 1,356 / (2 × 138 − 0.2 × 4.29) = 21.1 mm, plus 3 mm = 24.1 mm order 28 mm plate to allow for thinning when the head is formed
  4. MAWP of the 26 mm shell, corroded thickness 23 mm: P = 138 × 23 / (678 + 0.6 × 23) = 4.59 MPa = 45.9 barg
  5. Hydrotest at 1.3 × MAWP × stress ratio, taking the stress ratio as 1.0: nameplate MAWP 42.9 barg → test 55.8 barg nameplate MAWP 45.9 barg → test 59.6 barg

Rounding up the plate gave 7 % more pressure capacity than asked for. Stamping the vessel at the higher MAWP costs nothing now and can save a re-rating later, but it raises the test pressure and must agree with the relief valve set pressure.

Example 7. Fire-case relief load

Given
The Example 3 vessel on a skirt with its bottom tangent 1.2 m above grade, bare, with adequate drainage and firefighting. Wetted to LAHH (2.92 m above the bottom tangent), a conservative choice. Latent heat at relieving conditions taken as 280 kJ/kg.
Find
Heat input and relief rate.
  1. Height check: top of wetted zone = 1.2 + 2.92 = 4.12 m < 7.6 m → all of it counts
  2. Wetted area: 2:1 head ≈ 1.084 D² = 1.084 × 1.35² = 1.98 m² shell = π × 1.35 × 2.92 = 12.38 m² A_w = 14.36 m²
  3. Heat input, equation (19): Q = 43,200 × 1.0 × 14.36^0.82 = 43,200 × 8.89 = 384 kW
  4. Relief rate: W = 384,000 / 280,000 × 3,600 = 4,940 kg/h without drainage, equation (20): Q = 630 kW, W = 8,100 kg/h

The fire case is real but not large for this vessel. A blocked gas outlet with the upstream source still feeding would ask the valve to pass the full 12,500 kg/h of gas plus any vapour from the liquid, so fire is unlikely to set the orifice. It must still be documented, and it is the one case allowed 21 % accumulation instead of 10 %.

Example 8. Gas blowby into the low-pressure drum

Given
The Example 3 separator sends its liquid to the Example 4 drum through a level control valve with Cv = 25 and xT = 0.70. The downstream drum has a 10 barg design pressure and its relief valve relieves at 11 barg (12.0 bar a). Upstream gas: 40.0 bar a, 35.3 kg/m³, k = 1.27.
Find
The blowby gas flow if the separator loses its level with the valve fully open.
  1. Pressure ratio and choking, equation (21): x = (40.0 − 12.0)/40.0 = 0.700 F_γ = 1.27/1.40 = 0.907, F_γ x_T = 0.635 → x > 0.635, choked x = 0.635, Y = 1 − 0.635/(3 × 0.635) = 0.667
  2. Flow: W = 27.3 × 25 × 0.667 × √(0.635 × 40.0 × 35.3) = 455 × 29.9 = 13,600 kg/h
  3. Is the valve too big? The normal liquid duty, before allowing for flashing, needs only K_v = Q √(SG/Δp) = 30.0 × √(0.62/35) = 4.0, C_v = 1.156 K_v = 4.6 A valve with Cv = 10, still twice the normal requirement, would cut the blowby to 13,600 × 10/25 = 5,450 kg/h.

The blowby flow is more than twice the downstream drum’s normal gas flow of 6,000 kg/h, so it is likely to set that drum’s relief valve. The cheapest safeguard is often the one in step 3: do not oversize the level valve. An independent low-low level trip closing a shutdown valve is the other common answer.

Example 9. Reading a packaged separator capacity table

Given
The SEP-C row in Table 8: 42 in × 10 ft, 9,928 kPa working pressure; oil 2,289 m³/d at high level and 1,057 m³/d at low level for 1 min retention; gas 708,000 and 1,698,000 m³/d. Assume an inside diameter of 0.98 m, a 3.048 m shell, heads ignored, gas relative density 0.65 with Z = 0.85 at 38 °C, oil 800 kg/m³.
Find
The liquid fill behind each rating, and the K the gas ratings imply if they are quoted at full working pressure.
  1. Shell volume: A = π × 0.98²/4 = 0.754 m², V = 0.754 × 3.048 = 2.30 m³
  2. One minute of oil: high level: 2,289/1,440 = 1.590 m³ → 69 % of the shell (h/D ≈ 0.65) low level: 1,057/1,440 = 0.734 m³ → 32 % of the shell (h/D ≈ 0.36)
  3. Gas mass flow and actual volume at full working pressure: standard density = 0.65 × 1.223 = 0.795 kg/m³ low level: 1,698,000 × 0.795 / 86,400 = 15.6 kg/s ρ_G = 100.3e5 × 18.8 / (0.85 × 8,314 × 311.15) = 85.9 kg/m³ → Q = 0.182 m³/s
  4. Velocity and implied K: low level: gas area 0.68 × 0.754 = 0.513 m² → v = 0.355 m/s K = 0.355 / √((800 − 85.9)/85.9) = 0.123 m/s (0.40 ft/s) high level: gas area 0.233 m², v = 0.326 m/s, K = 0.113 m/s
  5. Consistency: the gas ratings differ by a factor of 2.40 and the gas areas by 2.21, so the two columns describe one vessel at two levels.

Read this way, the table is coherent: K of about 0.11–0.12 m/s is at or just below the bottom of API 12J’s horizontal range (0.122–0.152 m/s for a 3 m vessel). But that range is not derated for pressure the way GPSA’s mesh-pad values are, and at 99 barg a derated figure would be lower. The question to send the vendor is the one the sheet leaves open: at what pressure, gas density and liquid density are the gas ratings quoted, and with which internals?

Practice problems

Work each one before opening the solution. Answers are accepted within 2 % unless stated. Use g = 9.81 m/s² and R = 8,314 J/(kmol·K).

Problem 1. Mist-eliminator diameter

A vertical knockout drum with a mesh pad receives 2.0 m³/s of gas at actual conditions. Gas density 18 kg/m³, liquid density 700 kg/m³. Using K = 0.10 m/s, find the minimum mist-eliminator diameter.

m
V_max = 0.10 × √((700 − 18)/18) = 0.616 m/s A = 2.0 / 0.616 = 3.25 m², D = √(4 × 3.25/π) = 2.03 m

The vessel itself would be 2.25 m after the support-ring allowance and rounding up in 150 mm steps.

Problem 2. Pressure derating

What K does the GPSA rule give for a vertical drum with a horizontal mesh pad at 56 barg?

m/s
K = 0.107 − 0.003 × (56 − 7)/7 = 0.107 − 0.021 = 0.086 m/s

That is 20 % below the low-pressure value, on top of the density effect already in the Souders–Brown equation.

Problem 3. Gas density

Find the density of a natural gas with M = 18.5 kg/kmol and Z = 0.86 at 70 bar absolute and 30 °C.

kg/m³
ρ = 70e5 × 18.5 / (0.86 × 8,314 × 303.15) = 59.7 kg/m³

Using Z = 1 would understate the density by 14 % and oversize the vessel’s velocity limit.

Problem 4. Holdup and surge height

A vertical drum of 1.5 m inside diameter receives 30 m³/h of liquid. The specification asks for 8 min of holdup and 4 min of surge. What height is needed between LALL and LAHH?

m
A = π × 1.5²/4 = 1.767 m², Q_L = 0.5 m³/min h_H = 8 × 0.5/1.767 = 2.26 m, h_S = 4 × 0.5/1.767 = 1.13 m, total 3.40 m

With the fixed spacings this vessel will be over 5 m tall, H/D above 3.5: a candidate for a horizontal comparison.

Problem 5. Stokes’ law, and whether it applies

Find the Stokes terminal velocity of a 50 µm water droplet (998 kg/m³) in air at 20 °C (1.20 kg/m³, 0.0181 mPa·s). Is Stokes’ law valid here?

m/s
v = 9.81 × (50e-6)² × (998 − 1.2) / (18 × 1.81e-5) = 0.0750 m/s Re = 1.20 × 0.0750 × 50e-6 / 1.81e-5 = 0.25

Re is well below 1, so Stokes applies. The full correlation, equation (6), gives 0.070 m/s, 6 % lower, because even at Re = 0.25 the inertial term adds a little drag.

Problem 6. Segment area

What fraction of a horizontal drum’s cross-section lies below a liquid level at one quarter of the diameter?

–
θ = 2 arccos(1 − 2 × 0.25) = 2 arccos(0.5) = 2.094 rad f = (2.094 − sin 2.094)/(2π) = (2.094 − 0.866)/6.283 = 0.196

By symmetry, a level at three quarters of the diameter leaves the same fraction, 0.196, for the gas.

Problem 7. Inlet nozzle on momentum

A feed of 0.50 m³/s with a mean density of 45 kg/m³ enters through a vane-type inlet device limited to ρv² = 8,000 Pa. Find the minimum nozzle inside diameter. What would it be with no inlet device, limited to 1,400 Pa?

m
vane inlet: v = √(8,000/45) = 13.3 m/s → d = √(4 × 0.50/(π × 13.3)) = 0.219 m no device: v = √(1,400/45) = 5.58 m/s → d = 0.338 m

The inlet device lets the nozzle be a size or two smaller than the line, and more important, protects the droplet size distribution.

Problem 8. Shell thickness

Find the required shell thickness, including corrosion allowance, for a separator with design pressure 2.0 MPa, inside diameter 2,000 mm, SA-516-70 at 138 MPa, spot radiography (E = 0.85) and a 3 mm corrosion allowance.

mm
R = 1,000 + 3 = 1,003 mm t = 2.0 × 1,003 / (138 × 0.85 − 0.6 × 2.0) = 2,006 / 116.1 = 17.3 mm plus CA: 20.3 mm → order 22 mm

Full radiography (E = 1.0) would save about 3 mm of steel over the whole shell.

Problem 9. Fire-case relief rate

A bare vessel with adequate drainage and firefighting has 25 m² of wetted area below 7.6 m. The latent heat at relieving conditions is 300 kJ/kg. Find the relief rate.

kg/h
Q = 43,200 × 25^0.82 = 43,200 × 14.0 = 605 kW W = 605,000 / 300,000 × 3,600 = 7,260 kg/h

Doubling the wetted area raises the load by only 2^0.82 = 1.77 times.

Problem 10. Mesh pad turndown

A scrubber’s mesh pad is designed for 30,000 kg/h of gas at its design K. If the pad works down to 25 % of its design velocity, what is the lowest gas flow at the same pressure and temperature at which it still separates well?

kg/h
At constant density, velocity ∝ mass flow: 0.25 × 30,000 = 7,500 kg/h

If start-up or early-life flows are lower, a two-stage mist eliminator or a smaller boxed pad is the usual answer.

Problem 11. A binary flash

A feed of 40 mol % light component (K = 3.2) and 60 mol % heavy component (K = 0.45) is flashed. Find the vapour fraction V/F.

–
0.40 × 2.2/(1 + 2.2β) − 0.60 × 0.55/(1 − 0.55β) = 0 0.88(1 − 0.55β) = 0.33(1 + 2.2β) → 0.55 = 1.21β → β = 0.455

With two components Rachford–Rice reduces to a linear equation; with more, it needs iteration.

Problem 12. Is this scrubber overloaded?

An existing vertical scrubber of 1.2 m inside diameter with a full-diameter mesh pad now handles 0.55 m³/s of gas at actual conditions, 50 barg, ρG = 30 kg/m³, ρL = 600 kg/m³. What K is it running at? Compare it with the GPSA value at this pressure.

m/s
V = 0.55 / (π × 1.2²/4) = 0.486 m/s K = 0.486 / √((600 − 30)/30) = 0.112 m/s GPSA at 50 barg: 0.107 − 0.003 × 43/7 = 0.0886 m/s → 26 % over

Before replacing the vessel, ask a vendor to rate a high-capacity mesh, a mesh-and-vane combination or a cyclone deck in the existing shell.

Problem 13. Blowby flow

A level control valve with Cv = 40 and xT = 0.72 fails open on a separator at 30 bar a. Gas density upstream 25 kg/m³, k = 1.30. The downstream vessel relieves at 6 bar a. Find the blowby gas flow.

kg/h
x = (30 − 6)/30 = 0.80; F_γ = 1.30/1.40 = 0.929; F_γ x_T = 0.669 → choked x = 0.669, Y = 2/3 W = 27.3 × 40 × 0.667 × √(0.669 × 30 × 25) = 728 × 22.4 = 16,300 kg/h

Once the flow is choked, lowering the downstream pressure further adds nothing; raising the upstream pressure adds in proportion.

Problem 14. Hydrostatic test pressure

A vessel has an MAWP of 25 barg at a design temperature where the allowable stress is 118 MPa. At test temperature the allowable stress is 138 MPa. What is the standard hydrostatic test pressure?

barg
1.3 × 25 × 138/118 = 38.0 barg

The stress ratio raises the test for hot vessels, so that the test loads the steel as hard as the design condition does.

Problem 15. Settling length in a horizontal drum

In a horizontal drum the gas space above LAHH is 0.9 m deep. The design droplet falls at 0.20 m/s and the gas moves at 0.8 m/s. What is the minimum effective length?

m
fall time = 0.9/0.20 = 4.5 s; L = 0.8 × 4.5 = 3.6 m

Add the inlet and outlet zones: with Campbell’s L − D allowance, a 2 m drum would need about 5.6 m tangent to tangent.

Problem 16. What a vendor rating implies

A packaged horizontal separator of 0.98 m inside diameter and 3.048 m shell length is rated at 2,289 m³/d of oil with 1 min retention. Ignoring the heads, what fraction of the shell volume is liquid at that rating?

–
1 min of oil = 2,289/1,440 = 1.590 m³ shell = π × 0.98²/4 × 3.048 = 2.299 m³ → 0.69

About 69 % full, so the high-level rating leaves less than a third of the cross-section for gas. That is why its gas rating is lower.

What goes on the separator data sheet

The data sheet is where process design becomes a purchase. Everything a vendor needs to size, rate and guarantee the separator should be on it, for every operating case.

  • Service and cases. Tag, service, and every case: normal, maximum, minimum, start-up, upset, end of life.
  • Gas. Mass flow, molar mass, Z, density, viscosity at separator conditions.
  • Liquid. Mass and volume flow, density, viscosity, surface tension; a second liquid phase if present.
  • Conditions. Operating pressure and temperature; design pressure, design temperature and MDMT; vacuum design if needed.
  • Feed character. Flow regime in the inlet line, slug volume and frequency, solids, wax, foaming tendency, H₂S, CO₂, chlorides.
  • Performance. Allowable liquid carryover in the gas (US gal/MMscf or mg/Sm³) and gas carry-under in the liquid.
  • Liquid volumes. Holdup and surge times with their definitions; LALL, LAL, NLL, LAH and LAHH as heights and times.
  • Internals. Inlet device, mist eliminator type and material, vortex breakers, baffles, sand jetting, drain arrangements.
  • Nozzles. Schedule of sizes, flange classes and facings, services and orientations; manways.
  • Instruments. Level control and independent trip connections, bridles, pressure and temperature connections.
  • Relief. Relief valve set pressure and the governing case, with the fire and blowby cases noted.
  • Materials and construction. Material, corrosion allowance, sour service, post-weld heat treatment, insulation and heat tracing.
  • Codes. ASME VIII Division 1 (or the contract code), CSA B51 registration where the vessel is installed in Canada, API RP 12J and the owner’s standards.
  • Layout. Orientation, plot limits, elevation needed for the pump NPSH, skid or transport limits.
