Pumps for process engineers

How to choose the right type, calculate head and NPSH, read a vendor curve, apply API 610, ASME B73 and the Hydraulic Institute guidelines, and keep a pump from hurting anyone. A P&ID with its control system, nine worked examples and sixteen problems to solve.

A centrifugal pump does not choose its own flow. It settles wherever its head curve crosses the curve of the system it is connected to, and most of the pump problems I have seen in plants come back to someone forgetting that.

It starts with the energy balance, works through types, system curves, NPSH and curve reading, and ends with standards, safety and a set of problems. 01

Head, pressure and power

A pump adds mechanical energy to a liquid. Pump people express that energy per unit weight of liquid, which has units of length, and call it head. The total head of a pump is the change in the Bernoulli terms between its suction and discharge flanges:

H = (pd − ps)/(ρg) + (vd² − vs²)/(2g) + (zd − zs)(1)

p is static pressure (Pa), v the velocity in the nozzle (m/s), z the gauge elevation (m), ρ the density at pumping temperature (kg/m³), g = 9.81 m/s².

The reason we use head instead of pressure is that, for a given impeller and speed, a centrifugal pump develops the same head whatever the density of the liquid. The pressure rise it produces scales with density:

Δp = ρ g H(2)

So a pump that makes 50 m on water (4.9 bar) makes the same 50 m on gasoline at a relative density of 0.74, but only 3.6 bar. That is why pump datasheets carry head and the process engineer converts to pressure with the density at the pumping temperature.

The power the liquid receives, the shaft power the pump absorbs and the electrical power the motor draws are:

Ph = ρ g Q H    Pshaft = Ph/ηp    Pel = Pshaft/(ηm ηVFD)(3)

With Q in m³/h and H in m, a handy form is:

Pshaft [kW] = Q [m³/h] × H [m] × SG / (367 × ηp)(4)

The illustrative pump in Figure 1 at its BEP: 100 × 50 × 1.0 / (367 × 0.78) = 17.5 kW. Unlike head, power follows density, so the same pump on gasoline needs about 13 kW.

Head is in metres of the liquid being pumped. A gauge reads pressure. Convert with equation (2) using the density at the actual temperature, not at 15 °C.

The 367 in equation (4) is 3600 × 1000 / (1000 × 9.81).

Positive displacement pumps are the exception: they produce whatever pressure the system demands, so their duty is stated as flow and differential pressure.

02

Types of pumps and where they are used

There are two families, and the difference between them drives everything else on this page.

Rotodynamic (dynamic) pumps give the liquid velocity with an impeller and convert it to pressure in a volute or diffuser. Their flow depends on the system: close a discharge valve and the flow falls while the head rises to a finite shutoff value.

Positive displacement (PD) pumps trap a fixed volume and push it out every stroke or revolution. Their flow barely changes with pressure. Close the discharge and the pressure keeps rising until something breaks, which is why every PD pump needs a relief device.19

Choosing a type: the questions I ask first

  1. Is the liquid clean and thin? Start with a single-stage centrifugal. It is the default for most process duties.
  2. Is the head high for the flow? Calculate the specific speed (section 5). A very low value points to a multistage, a high-speed OH6, a regenerative turbine or a PD pump.
  3. How viscous is it at the coldest operating case? Centrifugal performance falls steadily with viscosity (section 8). For thick liquids, rotary PD pumps are usually more efficient and more predictable.
  4. Do I need a precise, adjustable flow? Use a metering pump to API 675.
  5. How bad is a leak? Toxic or flammable liquids with no tolerance for emissions point to dual pressurized seals under API 682 or a sealless pump.
  6. How much NPSH is there? Low NPSHa favours double-suction impellers, inducers or vertical can pumps, or a change to the layout.
  7. Are there solids, gas or shear-sensitive components? Consider recessed impellers, slurry pumps, progressive cavity or lobe pumps.

The system curve

The system curve is the head the piping demands at each flow. Write the energy balance from the liquid surface on the suction side to the liquid surface (or nozzle) on the discharge side:

Hsys(Q) = (p2 − p1)/(ρg) + (z2 − z1) + Σhf(Q) + Δpcv/(ρg)(5)

The first two terms do not depend on flow. Together they are the static head. The friction losses and the control valve drop are the dynamic head, roughly proportional to Q². Friction in each line segment comes from Darcy–Weisbach with a friction factor from Colebrook or the explicit Swamee–Jain form:

hf = (f L/D + ΣK) v²/(2g)     f = 0.25 / [log10(ε/(3.7D) + 5.74/Re0.9)]²(6)

For fittings I use the Crane-style resistance coefficients, K = n fT, where fT is the fully turbulent friction factor for the pipe size (0.019 for 2 in, 0.018 for 3 in, 0.017 for 4 in commercial steel):

Typical line velocities for clean liquids are about 1–2 m/s on suction and 1.5–3 m/s on discharge. Suction lines are usually one size larger than the pump nozzle. These are rules of thumb; use your company's line-sizing criteria.

Four things that catch people out

  1. The system curve is a family, not a line. Vessel levels and pressures change. Build the curve for the highest head case (low suction level, high discharge pressure) to size the pump, and the lowest head case to check run-out, NPSH and motor power.
  2. The control valve needs head to control. A common allowance is the larger of about 0.7 bar or a third of the dynamic losses at rated flow. Without it the valve runs wide open and the loop cannot control.
  3. Rated flow is not normal flow. Many companies rate the pump at 110–120 % of normal flow and carry a separate head margin. Stacking several margins is how pumps end up running at 50 % of BEP.
  4. Static and friction systems behave differently. A closed circulation loop is all friction; a lift into a pressurized vessel is mostly static. The difference decides whether variable speed saves energy and whether a second pump in parallel adds much flow. Try both presets in Figure 1.

04

NPSH and cavitation

As liquid enters the impeller eye it accelerates and turns onto the blades, and its static pressure falls below the pressure in the suction nozzle. If it falls below the vapour pressure, vapour bubbles form. They collapse a few millimetres later where the pressure has recovered. That is cavitation: noise like gravel, vibration, pitted blades, damaged seals and bearings, and eventually a loss of head.

Net positive suction head is the margin, in metres of liquid, between the total head at the pump inlet and the vapour pressure. The system provides NPSHa; the pump needs NPSHr, which manufacturers publish as NPSH3.

NPSHa = (psurface − pv)/(ρg) + z − hf,s(7)

psurface is the absolute pressure on the liquid surface, pv the vapour pressure at the highest pumping temperature, z the height of the liquid surface above the impeller eye (negative for a suction lift), hf,s the suction line losses at the flow being checked.

On a running pump you can measure it from a suction gauge at the nozzle, at elevation zg relative to the impeller eye:

NPSHa = (pgauge + patm − pv)/(ρg) + vs²/(2g) + zg(8)

NPSH3 is not a cavitation-free value. It is the NPSH at which cavitation has already reduced the first-stage head by 3 %.7 ANSI/HI 9.6.1 therefore recommends a margin, expressed as a difference (NPSHa − NPSH3) or a ratio (NPSHa/NPSH3), that depends on the service.7 Suppressing cavitation completely can need a ratio of 2 or much more, depending on inlet geometry and where the pump runs.9

Datum. NPSH is referred to the centreline of the impeller eye. For horizontal pumps that is the shaft centreline; for vertical pumps it is the eye of the first-stage impeller.

Liquids at their bubble point (reflux drums, column bottoms, LPG storage) have psurface = pv. The pressure term is zero, so NPSHa is just liquid height minus suction losses. Raising the vessel pressure does not help; raising the vessel does.

    Raising NPSHa or lowering NPSH3

    On the system side: raise the vessel or its minimum level, lower the pump (a pit or a vertical can pump), shorten and enlarge the suction line, remove fittings and reduced-port valves, keep strainers clean, subcool the liquid, or add a booster pump. Never throttle a suction valve to control flow.

    On the pump side: a lower speed, a double-suction impeller, an inducer, or an impeller with a larger eye all reduce NPSH3. The last option has a cost, described next.

    Suction specific speed

    S = N √Qeye / NPSH30.75(9)

    At BEP of the maximum impeller. US customary units are rpm, gpm and ft; Qeye is half the pump flow for a double-suction impeller.

    Low NPSH3 comes from a big impeller eye, and big eyes recirculate at part load. In 1982 J. L. Hallam studied 480 pumps over five years at the Amoco Texas City refinery and found that pumps with S above 11,000 (213 in metric units) failed about twice as often. Many specifications have used 11,000 as a hard limit since; later work argues the link is weaker for modern impeller designs, so treat it as a flag for a closer look at the minimum flow rather than an absolute rule.10

    The illustrative pump in Figure 1 has N = 3,550 rpm, Q = 440 gpm and NPSH3 = 16.4 ft at BEP, so S = 3,550 × √440 / 16.40.75 ≈ 9,100. Comfortable.

    Hydrocarbon mixtures boil over a range. The vapour pressure at the bubble point controls NPSH. A small heat input, from a hot day on the suction line or from recirculation, can move the bubble point enough to use up the margin.

    Entrained gas does not show up in equation (7) but reduces head and promotes cavitation-like damage. Vent suction lines at high points and keep vortex breakers in vessel outlets.

    Reading pump curves

    A vendor's performance curve is drawn for one speed and one pump size. It usually carries head against flow for several impeller diameters, lines of constant efficiency, the NPSH3 curve, shaft power on water, the minimum continuous flow and sometimes the allowable operating region. Figure 4 has all of these, numbered in the order I read them.

    Eight checks, keyed to Figure 4

    1. Plot the rated point (1). 90 m³/h at 44 m lies between the 230 and 250 mm curves. Solving 60d² − 0.001 × 90² = 44 for the diameter ratio gives d = 0.932, a 233 mm trim.
    2. Find BEP for the impeller as furnished (2). BEP flow scales with diameter: 100 × 0.932 = 93 m³/h. Rated flow is 97 % of BEP. API 610 requires rated flow within 80–110 % of BEP and prefers BEP between the rated and normal points.2
    3. Read efficiency from the iso-efficiency lines (3). About 76 % at the rated point.
    4. Check minimum flow (4). If the process can turn down below the minimum continuous stable flow, add a minimum-flow recycle (section 13).
    5. Read NPSH3 at rated flow and at the highest flow (5). 4.4 m at 90 m³/h, rising to 7 m at the end of the curve. Compare each with NPSHa plus the HI 9.6.1 margin.
    6. Read power and correct for density (6). 14.2 kW on water at the rated point. Size the motor so it is not overloaded at the end of the curve with the maximum impeller: 21.5 kW here. Multiply by relative density for other liquids.
    7. Check shutoff head for the downstream design pressure. API 610 defines maximum discharge pressure as maximum suction pressure plus the maximum differential the pump can develop with the furnished impeller at rated speed and specified relative density.4 Piping and the casing must hold it.
    8. Check head growth. API 610 asks for at least a 5 % head increase at rated conditions by fitting a larger impeller, among other means.4 Going from 233 to 250 mm gives (250/233)² = 1.15, a 15 % increase.

    Curve shape. A curve that rises continuously to shutoff is stable: each head has one flow. API 610 prefers stable curves and requires at least 10 % head rise from rated point to shutoff when pumps run in parallel.3 The 233 mm impeller in Figure 4 rises 18 % from its rated point to shutoff.

    Power curve shape depends on specific speed. Radial pumps take least power at shutoff, so they start against a closed or nearly closed discharge valve. Axial pumps take most power at shutoff and must start with the valve open.

    Operating regions

    Vibration, radial thrust and internal recirculation are lowest near BEP and grow as you move away. API 610 requires a preferred operating region (POR) of 70–120 % of BEP flow, with rated flow at 80–110 %. ANSI/HI 9.6.3 agrees on 70–120 % for most pumps1 and narrows it to 80–120 % for specific speeds above 4,500 in US units.13 The allowable operating region (AOR) is wider and is set by the manufacturer. It may be limited by temperature rise at low flow or NPSH at high flow, and the vendor must say which.2

    To the left of BEP: suction and discharge recirculation, higher radial loads and shaft deflection, temperature rise, seal and bearing wear. To the right: rising NPSH3, cavitation, higher velocities and erosion, and higher power. For overhung and between-bearings pumps up to 3,600 rpm and 300 kW per stage, API 610 limits bearing-housing vibration to 3.0 mm/s RMS in the POR and 3.9 mm/s elsewhere in the AOR.26

    Specific speed

    nq = N √Q / H0.75    (rpm, m³/s, m per stage, at BEP)(10)

    Specific speed classifies the impeller shape that suits a duty. In US units (rpm, gpm, ft) radial impellers run from about 500 to 4,000, mixed flow from 2,000 to 8,000 and axial flow from 7,000 to 20,000; below 500 a PD pump is usually the better choice.11 The US value is about 51.6 times the metric nq. The pump in Figure 1 has nq = 3,550 × √0.0278 / 500.75 = 31 (1,630 in US units): a radial impeller. Efficiency peaks at moderate specific speeds and falls off sharply at very low values, which is why small-flow, high-head duties are expensive to pump efficiently.

    Affinity laws, trimming and flow control

    For the same pump at a different speed, or the same casing with a slightly smaller impeller, performance scales with these ratios:

    Q2/Q1 = N2/N1   H2/H1 = (N2/N1)²   P2/P1 = (N2/N1)³(11)
    Q2/Q1 ≈ D2/D1   H2/H1 ≈ (D2/D1)²   P2/P1 ≈ (D2/D1)³(12)

    The speed laws are accurate for the same pump at constant efficiency. The diameter laws are approximate and get worse the more you trim, because the blade outlet geometry changes; use the vendor's tested curves for the final trim. NPSH3 rises roughly with the square of speed, but the exponent varies by design, so treat speed-scaled NPSH3 as an estimate.

    The most common mistake: the affinity laws move a point along a parabola through the origin, H = (H1/Q1²)Q². That parabola is the system curve only if there is no static head. With static head, 80 % speed does not give 80 % flow; Example 3 shows it giving 53 %.

    Trim in steps. Metal cannot be put back. Order a slightly larger trim than calculated, test, then trim further if needed.

    Trimming moves a pump towards lower specific speed and, beyond the vendor's minimum diameter, raises vibration and lowers efficiency.

      Parallel and series operation

      Pumps in parallel share a common suction and discharge header, so they run at the same head and their flows add. Pumps in series pass the same flow, and their heads add. Draw the combined curve by adding horizontally (parallel) or vertically (series), then intersect it with the system curve.

      How much a second pump helps depends entirely on the shape of the system curve. On a flat, static-dominated system a second pump in parallel adds a lot of flow. On a steep, friction-dominated system it adds very little, and series operation is what raises the flow. With the pump of Figure 1, at 100 m³/h for one pump:

      System (head at 100 m³/h = 50 m)Two in parallel, total flowGainEach pump, % of BEP
      Mostly static: 45 m static + 5 m friction141 m³/h+41 %71 %
      Mixed: 30 m static + 20 m friction115 m³/h+15 %58 %
      Friction only: 50 m friction107 m³/h+7 %53 %

      Design rules for parallel pumps

      • Check every combination: each pump alone, all pumps running, and the start-up of the second pump against the first. A pump running alone in a multi-pump system moves right on its curve, so check its NPSH and motor power at that flow.
      • Use pumps with stable curves and enough head rise to shutoff; API 610 requires at least 10 % from rated point to shutoff when parallel operation is specified.3 With flat curves, small differences between "identical" pumps let one pump push the other back towards shutoff.
      • Fit a check valve on each pump discharge so a running pump cannot drive flow backwards through an idle one and spin it in reverse.
      • Give each pump its own minimum-flow protection.

      Design rules for series pumps

      • The second pump's casing, seal chamber and suction flange see the first pump's discharge pressure. Rate them for it.
      • Start the upstream pump first, and trip the downstream pump on loss of the upstream one, or it will lose its suction.
      • Identical pumps in series on a friction system can be driven past the end of their curves. Select series pumps for the series duty.

      Viscous liquids

      Published curves are water tests. On a viscous liquid, disc friction and hydraulic losses grow, so flow, head and especially efficiency fall, and power rises. ANSI/HI 9.6.7 corrects water performance with three factors, CQ, CH and Cη, calculated from a single parameter B that combines viscosity, speed, head and flow at BEP.21 The 2010 edition applies to radial impellers handling Newtonian liquids between 1 and 4,000 cSt.22

      B = 16.5 ν0.5 HBEP0.0625 / (QBEP0.375 N0.25)   (ν cSt, H m, Q m³/h, N rpm)(13)
      CQ = exp[−0.165 (log10B)3.15]   CH = 1 − (1 − CQ)(QW/QBEP,W)0.75   Cη = B−0.0547 B0.69(14)

      Below B = 1 viscosity has no effect on the head curve. At B = 3 the head and flow corrections are under 3 % but efficiency falls almost 12 %; at B = 7 head and flow fall about 10 % and efficiency more than 30 %.23

      For the pump in Figure 1 on an oil at 200 cSt and relative density 0.90: B = 16.5 × 2000.5 × 500.0625 / (1000.375 × 3,5500.25) = 6.86, which gives CQ = CH,BEP = 0.910 and Cη = 0.672.

      The correction applies to water curves of the actual pump, and the method gives estimates. Ask the vendor for a viscous curve when B is large or the duty is critical, and check the cold-start case: viscosity at the lowest temperature sets starting torque and may set the motor size.

      The HI method is for Newtonian liquids. Slurries, polymer solutions and other non-Newtonian liquids need test data or the slurry standards.

      Positive displacement pumps

      Flow

      Q = ncyl × (π d²/4) × s × N × ηv    (reciprocating)(15)
      Q = Vd N − Qslip(Δp, μ)    (rotary)(16)

      ncyl plungers of diameter d and stroke s, crank speed N, volumetric efficiency ηv. In rotary pumps, slip back through the clearances grows with differential pressure and falls with viscosity, which is why gear and screw pumps get more efficient as liquids get thicker.

      Relief valves are not optional

      A PD pump has no shutoff head. Against a closed valve it keeps displacing until the line bursts or the pump fails, so a relief or safety valve on the discharge is essential.19 The rules I follow:

      • Install the relief valve between the pump and the first block valve, and size it for the full pump capacity at relieving conditions.
      • Account for accumulation: the pressure needed to pass full pump flow through the valve, typically 10–20 % above set pressure.20 Maximum system pressure is set pressure plus accumulation.
      • Route the discharge to the suction vessel, not the pump suction line, so recycled heat does not boil the liquid.
      • An integral relief valve in the pump casing protects the pump; the system usually needs its own external relief device as well.
      • Never start a PD pump against a closed discharge, and never control its flow with a discharge valve.

      Drivers, power and energy

      Size the motor for the worst case it will see, not only the rated point: the maximum relative density, the coldest (most viscous) liquid, and the end of the curve with the largest impeller that fits the casing. A motor that covers the whole curve is called non-overloading. API 610 sets the minimum motor rating as a percentage of rated pump power, with larger percentages for smaller motors; read the table in the edition you are buying to. For engineered clean-water pumps KSB suggests a 10–20 % margin over the power input at the operating point.39

      The pump in Figure 1 absorbs 17.5 kW at BEP but 21.5 kW at 130 m³/h. An 18.5 kW motor would be fine at rated flow and would trip if a downstream valve opened wide; a 22 kW motor covers the whole curve.

      Variable-speed drives

      A VFD saves the most where friction dominates and the flow varies. With high static head the savings shrink and two new problems appear: at low speed the shutoff head can fall below the static head, so the pump delivers nothing while still absorbing power, and the operating point slides left towards minimum flow. Set a minimum speed from the system curve, check the pump's lateral and torsional critical speeds across the speed range, and check motor cooling at low speed.

      Life-cycle cost

      For a pump that runs continuously, energy usually costs more over its life than the pump. The toluene pump in Example 2 draws about 8.4 kW of electricity at rated flow. At 8,000 hours a year that is 67 MWh, or about $6,700 a year at an illustrative $0.10/kWh. A few points of efficiency, or a pump selected close to BEP, pays for itself quickly.

      Hazardous areas

      Pumps handling flammable liquids usually sit in classified areas. In Canada the electrical equipment, including the motor and any instruments, follows the Canadian Electrical Code (CSA C22.1); elsewhere IEC 60079 or ATEX. The pump itself can be an ignition source through hot surfaces from dry running, a failing bearing or a rubbing seal; ISO 80079-36 and -37 cover non-electrical equipment for explosive atmospheres.

      Electrical power = shaft power / (motor efficiency × drive efficiency). Motor efficiency rises with size, from around 90 % for small motors to the mid-90s for large ones; a VFD adds a few per cent of losses. Use the motor data sheet.

      Safety: hazards and safeguards

      A process pump concentrates three hazards in one place: a rotating seal holding back the process fluid, a closed casing that can store a lot of energy, and an electric driver. Most of the safeguards below come up in every HAZOP; it is better to design them in than to be asked for them.

      Running blocked in

      If a centrifugal pump runs with both suction and discharge valves closed, all of its power goes into a few litres of trapped liquid. The liquid heats, expands and pressurizes the casing; the seal or casing can fail, and if the liquid is above its boiling point when it does, the release flashes violently, much like a boiler explosion. CCPS has published Beacons on water pumps that exploded this way, and notes that it can happen with any fluid.1516 Example 6 shows that the pump in Figure 1 could take a trapped casing of water from 25 to 100 °C in roughly ten minutes.

      AIChE's guidance is to monitor energy input, flow, power, pressure and temperature, to design control systems that shut the pump down in a deadhead, and to design so that an error does not become a catastrophe.17 Blocked-in starts often happen with spare pumps, auto-start pumps and remote starts, when the wrong pump is started or a valve is not lined up; check the line-up before every start and go to the pump if you are not sure.1516

      Minimum flow is two numbers. The thermal minimum keeps the temperature rise small; the hydraulic minimum (minimum continuous stable flow) keeps recirculation and vibration acceptable. The vendor gives the larger one, and the bypass must cover it.

      Temperature rise through the pump at low flow: ΔT = gH(1/η − 1)/cp.14

      Suction piping and installation

      API RP 686 asks for suction piping that delivers smooth, uniform flow to the nozzle: no tees, crosses, reduced-port valves or short-radius elbows near the suction, no high points to trap vapour, a straight run of typically five pipe diameters between the suction flange and the first elbow, valve, reducer or strainer, and a long-radius last elbow.37 Use eccentric reducers with the flat side up so vapour cannot collect. On double-suction pumps, keep the last elbow in the plane perpendicular to the shaft so both eyes get equal flow.

      Habits that prevent incidents

      • Before starting: line-up confirmed, casing full and vented, seal support system running, coupling guard on, rotation confirmed after any electrical work.
      • Start radial centrifugal pumps against a nearly closed discharge and open it promptly; never run them closed.
      • Keep standby pumps ready: warm (in hot service), full, isolations as the procedure states, auto-start tested.
      • Treat a temporary start-up strainer as temporary; track its differential pressure and remove it when the system is clean.
      • For maintenance: isolate both sides and the seal support system, depressure, drain to a closed system, flush or purge, and test for residual pressure before breaking a flange.

      The minimum-flow recycle

      A centrifugal pump that can be throttled to zero flow by its own control valve, by a closed downstream isolation valve or by a tripped reactor needs a way to keep its flow above the vendor's minimum. A recycle line from the discharge back to the suction source is the standard answer, and it is both a safety and a process control feature: it prevents the deadhead heating of section 12 and lets the forward flow be turned down to zero without starting and stopping the pump.

      Where it goes matters as much as having one:

      • Measure total pump flow. Put the minimum-flow flowmeter upstream of the recycle tee, so it sees forward plus recycled flow, and control that total, not the forward flow.
      • Return to the suction vessel, not the suction line. The tank inventory absorbs the heat; a short loop back to the pump suction does not.44 Example 9 shows the difference: about 0.7 K per hour in the tank, against about 11 K per minute in a 20 L suction loop.
      • Return below the liquid surface through a dip pipe or a low nozzle. It avoids flashing at the inlet and, for a static-accumulating liquid such as toluene, avoids splash filling. If the return must go into the vapour space, take the pressure drop in a multi-stage orifice.45
      • Let a restriction orifice take most of the pressure drop and leave the control valve a modest share, so the valve controls well and erodes slowly.
      • Make the minimum-flow valve fail open. Losing air then costs energy, not a pump.
      • Size for the worst case: the highest discharge pressure (lowest flow, highest suction pressure) and the lowest back pressure.
      • One protection per pump. For pumps that run in parallel, each needs its own flow measurement and recycle; a common one on the header cannot see whether one pump is being pushed back towards shutoff.

      Four methods are in common use. They all keep the total pump flow above the minimum; they differ in how much energy they waste and how much instrumentation they need.41

      How low is the minimum? Typical published minimum continuous flows are 25–40 % of BEP for low specific speed end-suction pumps and higher for high-energy and high specific speed designs.51 Always take the number from the vendor's curve for the pump you buy.

      Upstream and downstream: what sets the pump's operating point

      The pump in Figure 8 does not control anything by itself. Upstream, PCV-104 and the tank level fix its suction pressure; downstream, PIC-201 fixes the reactor pressure and FIC-108 chooses the flow. Together they place the operating point on the pump curve, exactly as in Figure 1. Change any one of them and the operating point moves, so the HAZOP has to ask what each one does when it fails.

      When the suction vessel is a process drum rather than a storage tank, the usual scheme is a cascade: the drum level controller sets the setpoint of the pump discharge flow controller, so flow disturbances are corrected quickly and level is corrected slowly. Boiler drums use the same idea, with a level controller cascaded into a feedwater flow controller.49 The minimum-flow loop then sits underneath, unchanged.

      The spare pump and auto-start

      Pressure-triggered auto-start is the most common arrangement because a pressure transmitter is cheaper and more robust than a flowmeter. The setpoint is a compromise: low enough that a normal dip, such as the running pump moving to run-out, does not start the spare, and high enough that a real trip always does.46 Two details are easy to miss. The spare must be lined up, full and, in hot service, warm, or the auto-start starts a dry or blocked-in pump. And the trips that stop a pump for a process reason, such as low-low level or a closed suction valve, must block the auto-start, or the spare starts into the same problem a few seconds later.

      Emergency isolation

      A large inventory of flammable or toxic liquid connected to a pump is a hazard if a seal or a flange fails, because nobody can safely walk into a pump fire to shut a manual valve. A remotely operated shutoff valve on the vessel outlet lets the operator isolate the inventory from the control room; the UK HSE's HSG244 sets out how to decide whether one is needed and how to specify it.47 Refiners often place them in pump suction lines, for example for liquids above their autoignition temperature or with high H₂S, and rate them for the pump shutoff pressure because a leaking check valve can expose them to discharge pressure.48 Closing the valve on a running pump starves it, so its closed position switch trips the pump.

      A P&ID review checklist for a pump

      • Suction: vessel elevation and low-low level support the NPSH calculation at maximum flow; no high points; straight run and long-radius elbow at the nozzle; temporary strainer with a way to see its pressure drop.
      • Each pump: suction and discharge isolation, a check valve upstream of the discharge isolation valve, a discharge pressure gauge, vent and drain to a closed system for hazardous liquids.
      • Minimum flow: flowmeter on total pump flow, recycle to the suction vessel, valve fail-safe position, orifice, low-flow trip as backup.
      • Seal: the API 682 arrangement and piping plan for the hazard, with its alarms routed to the control room.
      • Pressure: piping and equipment up to the first relief device rated for maximum suction pressure plus shutoff head with the largest impeller and highest density; thermal relief where liquid can be blocked in.
      • Spare: auto-start logic, the trips that block it, and how the operator knows which pump is lined up.
      • Downstream: control valve fail position chosen for the process, check valve against reverse flow from a pressurized receiver, shutdown valve for the receiver's trips.

      Minimum-flow return and level control. Because the recycle goes back to the same tank, it does not change the tank's level balance; only the forward flow does. If the recycle went to a different vessel, the level controllers of both vessels would see it.

      Example 1. NPSH for a reflux pump on a drum at its bubble point

      Given
      Reflux drum at 1,200 kPa(a), liquid at its bubble point. Low-low liquid level 4.5 m above the pump centreline. Suction losses 0.6 m at rated flow. Vendor NPSH3: 2.8 m at rated flow, 3.5 m at 120 % of rated. Hydrocarbon service.
      Find
      Whether NPSHa meets ANSI/HI 9.6.1 at rated flow and at 120 % flow.
      1. The liquid is saturated, so psurface = pv and the pressure term in equation (7) is zero.
      2. At rated flow: NPSHa = 0 + 4.5 − 0.6 = 3.9 m
      3. Required, hydrocarbon process (ratio 1.1 or 1.0 m, whichever is greater): max(1.1 × 2.8, 2.8 + 1.0) = max(3.08, 3.80) = 3.80 m ≤ 3.9 m → acceptable, just
      4. At 120 % flow, losses scale with Q²: hf = 0.6 × 1.2² = 0.86 m; NPSHa = 4.5 − 0.86 = 3.64 m
      5. Required at 120 %: max(1.1 × 3.5, 3.5 + 1.0) = 4.50 m > 3.64 m → not acceptable
      6. Liquid height needed: z ≥ 4.50 + 0.86 = 5.36 m Raise the drum by about 0.9 m, choose a pump with lower NPSH3 at high flow, or limit the maximum flow.

      NPSHa falls and NPSH3 rises as flow increases. Check at the highest flow the pump will see, with the lowest level, and remember that pressurizing a bubble-point drum adds nothing.

      Example 3. Variable speed against throttling

      Given
      Illustrative pump; system H = 30 + 0.002Q². At full speed the pump delivers 100 m³/h. The process now needs 80 m³/h for most of the year.
      Find
      Shaft power by throttling and by speed reduction, and what 80 % speed actually delivers.
      1. System head at 80 m³/h: Hsys = 30 + 0.002 × 80² = 42.8 m
      2. Throttling at full speed: Hpump = 60 − 0.001 × 80² = 53.6 m; x = 0.80; η = 0.78(2x − x²) = 74.9 % P = 1000 × 9.81 × (80/3600) × 53.6 / 0.749 = 15.6 kW The valve burns 53.6 − 42.8 = 10.8 m.

      The trimmed diameter and the power saved.
      1. Draw the trim parabola through the new duty: H = (42/100²) Q² = 0.0042 Q²
      2. Intersect it with the full-diameter curve: 60 − 0.001Q² = 0.0042Q² → Q1 = 107.4 m³/h, H1 = 48.5 m
      3. Diameter law: D2 = 250 × 100/107.4 = 232.7 mm (check: 250 × √(42/48.5) = 232.7 mm)
      4. Order 235 mm, test, then trim further if needed.
      5. Power: throttled full impeller at 100 m³/h absorbs 17.5 kW at 50 m. Trimmed, at 42 m and about 77.6 %: P = 9.81 × (100/3600) × 42 / 0.776 = 14.8 kW → saving 2.7 kW

      Do not scale diameter by the head ratio at constant flow; scale along the parabola through the origin. Before trimming, recheck NPSH3, minimum flow and the 5 % head-growth margin you will give up.

      Example 5. Adding a second pump in parallel

      Given
      One illustrative pump on the system H = 30 + 0.002Q² delivers 100 m³/h. Production wants more and an identical spare is available.
      Find
      Flow with both running and where each pump operates.
      1. Combined curve for two identical pumps: at head H each delivers q, so Q = 2q: H = 60 − 0.001 (Q/2)² = 60 − 0.00025 Q²
      2. Intersect with the system: 60 − 0.00025Q² = 30 + 0.002Q² → Q = 115.5 m³/h, H = 56.7 m
      3. Each pump: q = 57.7 m³/h = 58 % of BEP; η = 0.78(2 × 0.577 − 0.577²) = 64 %

      A 15 % gain, with both pumps outside their preferred region. The fixes are a pump selected for the two-pump duty, impellers matched to the higher head, or a different arrangement. See Figure 5.

      Example 6. How fast a blocked-in pump heats up

      Given
      The illustrative pump is started with both valves closed. Its power curve gives 10.5 kW at zero flow. The casing holds about 15 L of water at 25 °C and about 60 kg of wetted steel (cp 0.5 kJ/kg·K).
      Find
      The heating rate and time to 100 °C, ignoring heat loss to air; then the temperature rise at 30 % of BEP flow.
      1. Liquid only: dT/dt = 10,480 / (15 × 4,180) = 0.167 K/s ≈ 10 K/min → 25 to 100 °C in about 7.5 min
      2. With the steel heated too: dT/dt = 10,480 / (15 × 4,180 + 60 × 500) = 0.113 K/s ≈ 6.8 K/min → about 11 min
      3. At 30 % of BEP flow (30 m³/h, H = 59.1 m, η = 39.8 %), the liquid passes through and the rise is small:14 ΔT = 9.81 × 59.1 × (1/0.398 − 1) / 4,180 = 0.21 K

      With flow, the temperature rise is a fraction of a kelvin; without it, a casing can reach boiling in minutes. For a hydrocarbon near its bubble point, even 1–2 K of heating at low flow can use up the NPSH margin.

      Example 7. Acceleration head for a triplex plunger pump

      Given
      Triplex plunger pump injecting lean amine at 40 L/min, crank speed 120 rpm. Suction line 6 m of 1 in Sch 40 (26.6 mm bore). K = 1.5.
      Find
      Acceleration head, and the effect of a simplex pump or a 2 in suction line.
      1. Velocity: V = (40/60,000) / (π × 0.0266²/4) = 1.20 m/s
      2. Triplex, C = 0.066: ha = 6 × 1.20 × 120 × 0.066 / (1.5 × 9.81) = 3.9 m
      3. Simplex, C = 0.400: ha = 23.4 m
      4. Triplex on 2 in Sch 40 (52.5 mm bore, V = 0.31 m/s): ha = 1.0 m

      Acceleration head is subtracted from NPSHa and can exceed every other suction term. More plungers and larger, shorter suction lines are the cheapest cures. See Figure 7.

      Example 8. The same pump on a viscous oil

      Given
      Illustrative pump, water BEP 100 m³/h at 50 m and 78 %, 3,550 rpm. Oil at 200 cSt, relative density 0.90.
      Find
      Viscous BEP and shaft power using ANSI/HI 9.6.7.
      1. Parameter B, equation (13): B = 16.5 × 14.14 × 1.277 / (5.623 × 7.719) = 6.86
      2. Correction factors, equation (14): CQ = exp[−0.165 × (0.8367)3.15] = 0.910; CH at BEP = 0.910; Cη = 6.86−0.0547 × 6.860.69 = 0.672
      3. Viscous BEP: Q = 91.0 m³/h; H = 45.5 m; η = 0.672 × 78 % = 52.4 %
      4. Power: P = 900 × 9.81 × (91.0/3600) × 45.5 / 0.524 = 19.4 kW The water curve corrected only for density would suggest 15.7 kW.

      Viscosity hits efficiency and power much harder than head. Size the motor for the coldest, thickest case, and compare against a rotary PD pump when B is high.

      Example 9. Sizing the minimum-flow recycle for the toluene pump

      Given
      The pump of Example 2 and Figure 8. The (illustrative) vendor curve is H = 72 − 0.00918Q², rated 33 m³/h at 62 m, with a minimum continuous stable flow of 10 m³/h at 71.1 m and 30 % efficiency. Toluene ρ = 862 kg/m³, cp = 1.70 kJ/kg·K. Tank at atmospheric pressure, minimum level 1.5 m above the pump centreline. The recycle is 1½ in Sch 40 (40.9 mm bore), 25 m equivalent length, entering the tank through a nozzle 0.3 m above the pump centreline.
      Find
      The pressure drop available to FV-107 and RO-107, the orifice bore, and where the heat goes.
      1. Suction and discharge pressure at 10 m³/h (suction losses negligible): p_s = 101.3 + 862 × 9.81 × 1.5/1000 = 114.0 kPa(a) p_d = 114.0 + 862 × 9.81 × 71.1/1000 = 715.1 kPa(a)
      2. Back pressure at the return nozzle, with 1.2 m of liquid above it at minimum level: 101.3 + 862 × 9.81 × 1.2/1000 = 111.5 kPa(a); nozzle elevation 0.3 m = 2.5 kPa
      3. Recycle line friction at 10 m³/h: v = 2.12 m/s; Re = 1.33×10⁵; f = 0.0222 h_f = 0.0222 × 25/0.0409 × 2.12²/(2 × 9.81) = 3.1 m = 26.2 kPa
      4. Pressure drop left for the valve and orifice: 715.1 − 111.5 − 2.5 − 26.2 = 574.9 kPa
      5. Give FV-107 about 100 kPa at full recycle for control, and the orifice the rest, 475 kPa. Orifice bore with Cd = 0.61: A = (10/3600) / [0.61 × √(2 × 475,000/862)] = 1.37×10⁻⁴ m² → d = 13.2 mm, β = 0.32
      6. The orifice drops 475 kPa into a downstream pressure of only about 140 kPa(a). A single plate with that ratio is likely to cavitate, so specify a multi-stage orifice (three plates of about 160 kPa each) and confirm with the orifice supplier.45
      7. Heat. All the power at minimum flow ends up in the liquid: P = 862 × 9.81 × (10/3600) × 71.1 / 0.30 = 5.57 kW ΔT per pass = 5,570 / (2.394 kg/s × 1,700) = 1.4 K
      8. Returned to T-101 with 20 m³ (17.2 t) at minimum level: 5,570 × 3,600 / (17,240 × 1,700) = 0.7 K per hour Returned to a 20 L loop at the pump suction instead, the same liquid passes every 7 s: 1.4 K per pass ≈ 11 K per minute, with nothing to remove it

      The recycle is cheap insurance: a 1½ in line, a small valve and a stack of orifice plates keep the pump alive at zero forward flow. Its destination matters as much as its size. Into the tank, the heat is negligible; into the suction line, it boils the pump within minutes.

      Practice problems

      Work each one before opening the solution. Answers are accepted within 2 %. Where a problem uses "the illustrative pump", it means H = 60 − 0.001Q², η = 0.78(2x − x²) with x = Q/100, at 3,550 rpm.

      Problem 1. Pressure to head

      A pump raises 50 % ethylene glycol (relative density 1.07) by 8.5 bar. What head does it develop? What differential pressure would the same pump give on water (relative density 0.998)?

      m
      H = 850,000 / (1,070 × 9.81) = 81.0 m. On water: Δp = 998 × 9.81 × 81.0 = 7.93 bar.

      Same head, lower pressure, lower power.

      Problem 2. Suction lift with warm water

      A pump lifts water at 60 °C (ρ = 983 kg/m³, pv = 19.95 kPa) from an open sump at sea level. The water surface is 3.0 m below the impeller eye and suction losses are 0.8 m. Find NPSHa.

      m
      (101,325 − 19,950)/(983 × 9.81) − 3.0 − 0.8 = 8.44 − 3.80 = 4.64 m

      At 60 °C, vapour pressure has taken almost 2 m off the atmospheric term. Hot water and suction lift do not mix well.

      Problem 3. Butane sphere at low level

      A butane transfer pump takes suction from a sphere at the vapour pressure of its contents. At normal level the liquid is 6.0 m above the pump centreline; suction losses are 1.2 m. What is NPSHa if operators draw the level down to 2.0 m above the centreline?

      m
      Normal: 6.0 − 1.2 = 4.8 m. Low level: 2.0 − 1.2 = 0.8 m.

      The pressure term is zero for a saturated liquid. The safeguard is a low-level trip set from the NPSH calculation, not from the tank's mechanical minimum.

      Problem 4. A 60 Hz pump on a 50 Hz supply

      A pump rated 200 m³/h at 30 m and 22 kW at 1,780 rpm is moved to a site where the motor will run at 1,480 rpm. Estimate the new flow, head and shaft power at the equivalent point. Enter the power.

      kW
      r = 1480/1780 = 0.831; Q = 166 m³/h; H = 30 × 0.691 = 20.7 m; P = 22 × 0.575 = 12.6 kW

      The pump loses 31 % of its head. Whether it still meets the duty depends on the system curve, which is the real question to ask.

      Problem 5. Two pumps in series

      Two illustrative pumps run in series on the system H = 30 + 0.002Q². Find the flow. Is that acceptable for the pumps?

      m³/h
      2(60 − 0.001Q²) = 30 + 0.002Q² → 90 = 0.004Q² → Q = 150 m³/h, H = 75 m

      Each pump runs at 150 % of BEP, beyond the end of its curve at 130 %: cavitation and motor overload. Series pumps must be selected for the series duty.

      Problem 6. A non-overloading motor

      The illustrative pump is rated at its BEP (17.5 kW on water) but its curve runs to 130 m³/h. What shaft power does it absorb at the end of the curve, and which IEC motor size (15, 18.5, 22, 30 kW) is non-overloading?

      kW
      H = 60 − 0.001 × 130² = 43.1 m; x = 1.3; η = 0.78(2.6 − 1.69) = 0.710 P = 9.81 × (130/3600) × 43.1 / 0.710 = 21.5 kW → 22 kW motor

      An 18.5 kW motor covers the rated point only.

      Problem 7. Temperature rise at minimum flow

      A hydrocarbon pump (cp = 2.2 kJ/kg·K) develops 150 m at its minimum flow, where its efficiency is 30 %. Find the temperature rise through the pump.

      K
      ΔT = 9.81 × 150 × (1/0.30 − 1) / 2,200 = 1.56 K

      Check what 1.6 K does to the vapour pressure of the recycled liquid if the bypass returns to the suction line instead of the vessel.

      Problem 8. Diaphragm pump suction

      A single-acting duplex diaphragm pump (C = 0.200) runs at 90 strokes per minute and draws water at 20 °C (ρ = 998 kg/m³, pv = 2.34 kPa) from an atmospheric tank with its surface 2.0 m above the pump. The suction line is 10 m long with a mean velocity of 0.8 m/s and 0.5 m of losses. Use K = 1.4. Find the NPSHa left after acceleration head.

      m
      Static NPSHa = (101,325 − 2,339)/(998 × 9.81) + 2.0 − 0.5 = 11.61 m h_a = 10 × 0.8 × 90 × 0.200 / (1.4 × 9.81) = 10.48 m; net = 1.1 m

      Almost all the margin is gone. A suction stabilizer, a shorter or larger line, or a triplex would fix it.

      Problem 9. A low specific speed duty

      A duty needs 25 m³/h at 120 m. Calculate nq for a single-stage pump at 3,550 rpm, and for a two-stage pump. Enter the single-stage value.

      –
      n_q = 3550 × √(25/3600) / 120^0.75 = 3550 × 0.0833 / 36.3 = 8.2 (about 420 in US units) Two stages of 60 m: n_q = 13.7

      At about 420 in US units a single-stage radial pump is in the region where PD pumps are often considered, and its efficiency will be poor. A two-stage pump, a high-speed OH6, or a PD pump is worth pricing.

      Problem 10. Is this vendor's selection acceptable?

      Normal flow is 70 m³/h and rated flow 80 m³/h. The vendor offers an API 610 pump whose BEP with the furnished impeller is 105 m³/h. Rated flow as a percentage of BEP?

      %
      80/105 = 76 % (outside 80–110 %); normal 70/105 = 67 % (outside the 70–120 % POR)

      Reject it, or ask for a smaller pump or impeller. The pump would spend its life left of the preferred region.

      Problem 11. Chemical pump margin

      A chemical process pump has NPSH3 = 3.2 m and the system gives NPSHa = 3.6 m. Using the conservative end of the HI chemical range (ratio 1.2 or 1.0 m), what minimum NPSHa is recommended?

      m
      max(1.2 × 3.2, 3.2 + 1.0) = max(3.84, 4.20) = 4.2 m. Present: ratio 1.13, margin 0.4 m.

      It fails even the low end of the range (max(1.1 × 3.2, 3.2 + 0.6) = 3.8 m).

      Problem 12. Triplex plunger capacity

      A triplex pump has 40 mm plungers, a 75 mm stroke and runs at 180 rpm with 95 % volumetric efficiency. Find the delivered flow.

      m³/h
      Q = 3 × (π × 0.040²/4) × 0.075 × 180 × 60 × 0.95 = 2.90 m³/h

      The relief valve downstream must pass all 2.90 m³/h at relieving pressure.

      Problem 13. Where does the relief valve go?

      A gear pump discharges through a filter, a block valve and a flowmeter to a blending header. Where should the relief valve be, what capacity should it have, and where should it discharge?

      Between the pump and the first device that can block flow, here upstream of the filter (which can plug) and the block valve. Size it for the full pump capacity at relieving conditions, with the set pressure and accumulation below the design pressure of the pump and piping. Route it to the suction vessel, not the pump suction line. The pump's internal relief valve, if fitted, protects the pump only.

      Problem 14. The suction valve trick

      An operator controls a centrifugal pump by pinching the suction valve "because it is closer". Explain what happens and what to do instead.

      Throttling the suction adds a loss term to equation (7), so NPSHa falls while NPSH3 stays the same. The pump cavitates, the head drops erratically, and the seal and bearings suffer. It can also flash the liquid in the suction line. Control on the discharge, with a variable-speed drive, or with a bypass to the suction vessel, and keep suction valves fully open.

      Problem 15. A restriction orifice for a water pump

      A water pump (ρ = 998 kg/m³) needs a minimum-flow recycle of 12 m³/h. After the control valve and line losses, 3.0 bar is left for a single-plate restriction orifice with Cd = 0.61. What bore does the orifice need?

      mm
      A = (12/3600) / [0.61 × √(2 × 300,000/998)] = 0.003333 / 14.96 = 2.23×10⁻⁴ m² d = √(4A/π) = 16.8 mm

      Check the downstream pressure before accepting a single plate: if the ratio across it is high, use several plates in series.

      Problem 16. The auto-start that made it worse

      In a HAZOP of Figure 8, someone proposes deleting the "block standby auto-start" column from Table 4 to simplify the logic. What happens on a low-low tank level if you agree?

      LSLL-102 trips P-101A, discharge pressure falls, and PALL-106 starts P-101B through I-2. The spare now runs on a tank that is still at low-low level, so it loses NPSH margin, cavitates and may run dry, which can fail its seal and release toluene. Any trip that stops a pump for a process reason (low-low level, closed suction valve, low-low flow) must also block the auto-start; only faults of the pump itself (motor, seal, vibration) should start the spare.

      What goes on the datasheet

      The process engineer owns the process side of the datasheet. A pump is only as good as the data it was selected for, so I fill in every line below before a vendor sees it.

      • Liquid: name and composition, including trace and dissolved components
      • Temperatures: normal, minimum and maximum pumping temperature
      • Properties at each temperature: density, viscosity, vapour pressure, specific heat
      • Solids: concentration, size, hardness; entrained gas
      • Hazards: flammability, toxicity, autoignition temperature, corrosion, polymerization or crystallization
      • Flows: minimum, normal and rated, with the basis for each margin
      • Suction pressure: minimum, normal and maximum, with vessel levels
      • Discharge pressure: at rated flow, and the maximum the system can impose
      • NPSHa: value, datum (impeller eye or grade) and the flow it was calculated at
      • Operation: continuous or intermittent, parallel or series, spare philosophy, auto-start
      • Standards and seals: API 610 or ASME B73, API 682 arrangement and plan, or sealless
      • Materials: casing and impeller class for the corrosion and erosion duty
      • Driver: motor or turbine, voltage, VFD, area classification
      • Instruments: minimum-flow protection and its method (section 13), trips and alarms, vibration and temperature monitoring
      • Site: elevation, ambient range, winterization (heat tracing, insulation)
      • Testing: performance and NPSH tests, acceptance grade, witness points
