Heat exchangers: types, sizing and the standards behind them

How the common exchanger types differ, how to size one by hand well enough to check a vendor, which codes govern the hardware, and five worked examples from refinery, utility and carbon-capture service.

Alireza Lotfollahzade, EIT Updated 15 November 2026 About 35 min read Standard editions checked 15 November 2026

Every exchanger, from a kitchen radiator to a crude preheat train, is sized with the same three ideas: an energy balance, a driving temperature difference, and a conductance that tells you how easily heat crosses the wall.

I keep re-deriving these on project after project, so this page puts the method, the hardware and the paperwork in one place. The hand methods here are for screening and for checking the output of rigorous tools such as HTRI or Aspen EDR. They do not replace them.

Q = ṁh·cp,h·(T1 − T2) = ṁc·cp,c·(t2 − t1)(1)
Q = U · A · F · ΔTlm(2)
ΔTlm = (ΔTa − ΔTb) / ln(ΔTa/ΔTb),   ΔTa = T1 − t2,  ΔTb = T2 − t1(3)

Capital T is the hot stream and lower-case t the cold stream; subscript 1 is the inlet and 2 the outlet. Equation (3) is the log-mean temperature difference for pure counter-current flow. Real shell-and-tube units are rarely pure counter-current, so the correction factor F (≤ 1) in equation (2) accounts for the mixed flow pattern. F is read from charts, or calculated, from two dimensionless ratios:

P = (t2 − t1) / (T1 − t1)    R = (T1 − T2) / (t2 − t1)(4)

P is the thermal effectiveness of the cold side and R is the ratio of heat-capacity rates (ṁcp cold over ṁcp hot). When one side condenses or boils at constant temperature, R or P goes to zero and F = 1 for every arrangement.

The overall coefficient U

U is a series of resistances: the two film coefficients, the two fouling layers and the tube wall. Referred to the outside area of a plain tube:

1/Uo = 1/ho + Rf,o + Do·ln(Do/Di)/(2kw) + Rf,i·Do/Di + Do/(Di·hi)(5)

The largest term controls the design. If the shell-side film is 60 % of the total resistance, a better tube-side correlation changes nothing; a different baffle arrangement might. Example 1 below works through the numbers.

When the outlet temperatures are unknown: ε–NTU

LMTD suits design, where you know all four temperatures and want the area. For rating an existing exchanger (known area, new flows), use the effectiveness–NTU form, which avoids iterating on outlet temperatures:

ε = Q / [Cmin·(T1 − t1)],   NTU = U·A / Cmin,   Cr = Cmin/Cmax(6)

For each flow arrangement there is a closed-form ε(NTU, Cr). For counter-current flow ε = [1 − e−NTU(1−Cr)] / [1 − Cr·e−NTU(1−Cr)]. The calculator on this page uses these relations to compute F exactly, rather than reading it off a chart.

Exchanger types

Choose the type from the service: pressure, temperature, fouling, how close the temperatures approach, whether a fluid changes phase, and whether you need to open it for cleaning. The shell-and-tube exchanger remains the default in refining and petrochemicals because it tolerates high pressure, dirty fluids and thermal cycling, and because every fabricator can build and repair it.

Shell-and-tube and the TEMA designation

TEMA names a shell-and-tube exchanger with three letters: front head, shell, rear head. An AES has a channel with a removable cover (A), a one-pass shell (E) and a split-ring floating head (S). The floating head lets the bundle expand freely and lets you pull it for cleaning both sides, which is why it is common in fouling hydrocarbon service. Other combinations you will see on datasheets:

  • BEM / AEL / NEN: fixed tubesheets. The cheapest construction, with no internal gasketed joint, but the shell side cannot be cleaned mechanically and the shell-to-tube temperature difference is limited unless an expansion joint is added.
  • BEU / AEU: U-tubes. One tubesheet, free expansion, suited to high pressure and large temperature differences. The inside of the U-bends is hard to clean mechanically, so keep fouling fluids on the shell side.
  • AES / AET / AEP / AEW: floating heads. Removable bundle and free expansion, at the cost of internal gaskets and a larger shell-to-bundle clearance.
  • AKT / BKU: kettle (K shell) reboilers, with a large vapour space above the bundle for disengagement.
  • F, G, H, J and X shells: two-pass shells, split flow, divided flow and cross flow. J and X shells give low shell-side pressure drop for condensers and gas coolers.

TEMA also sets three mechanical classes. Class R is the most demanding, for petroleum and related processing; Class C is for general commercial service; Class B is for chemical process service. Class R asks for a larger corrosion allowance, heavier minimum shell and baffle thicknesses, and more and larger tie rods, so it costs more.

The other families

  • Double-pipe and multitube hairpin. One or a few tubes inside a pipe, bent into a hairpin. True counter-current flow, cheap in small duties, easy to add in series. Good for high-pressure, small-flow services and for temperature crosses that a 1–2 shell cannot handle.
  • Gasketed plate-and-frame (PHE). Corrugated plates clamped in a frame. Very high film coefficients, close approach temperatures (a few °C), compact, expandable by adding plates. Limited by gasket pressure and temperature ratings and by its narrow channels, which do not suit fibrous or heavily fouling fluids.
  • Welded and semi-welded plate, plate-and-shell. Plates welded in pairs or into a pack inside a shell, to remove some or all gaskets for aggressive fluids or higher pressure, at the cost of mechanical cleaning on the welded side.
  • Brazed aluminium plate-fin (BAHX). Stacks of finned passages brazed into a block. Enormous surface per volume and many streams in one core. Used in cryogenic air separation and LNG cold boxes. Clean, dry, mercury-free fluids only.
  • Spiral plate. Two long plates rolled into a spiral, giving a single curved channel per fluid. Tolerates slurries and fouling fluids because the high local velocity scours the channel.
  • Air-cooled (fin-fan). Finned tube bundles with fans, forced or induced draught. No cooling water, so they are standard for refinery overhead condensers and product coolers where water is scarce or expensive. They need plot space, and the outlet temperature is limited by the summer design air temperature.
  • Diffusion-bonded and printed-circuit (PCHE). Chemically etched channels in plates, diffusion-bonded into a solid block. Very high pressure in a compact block, used for gas processing, supercritical CO₂ cycles and hydrogen precooling. Fine channels need clean fluids and good filtration.

Sizing a shell-and-tube unit

Thermal design is iterative: you guess a geometry, rate it, and adjust until area, pressure drop and velocity are all acceptable. The steps below are the order I use when checking a vendor's offer or preparing a process datasheet.

  1. Fix the duty from the heat and material balance with equation (1). For condensing or boiling streams, build a heat-release curve (temperature against enthalpy); a single LMTD over a curved profile can be badly wrong.
  2. Allocate the fluids. Put on the tube side whatever is corrosive, fouling, at high pressure, or is cooling water; tubes are cheaper to make from alloy, easier to clean and cheaper to design for pressure. Put viscous liquids and condensing vapours on the shell side, where baffles create turbulence at lower Reynolds numbers.
  3. Compute ΔTlm, P, R and F. If F for one 1–2 shell falls below about 0.75–0.8, the design sits on the steep part of the curve and a small change in temperatures causes a large change in area. Add shells in series or use a counter-current type.
  4. Estimate U from Table 1 or experience, and get a first area from equation (2).
  5. Choose geometry: tube OD (19.05 mm and 25.4 mm are standard), gauge, length (often 6.1 m), pitch and layout (30° triangular for clean shell side, 90° or 45° square for mechanical cleaning), passes to give the target tube velocity, baffle cut and spacing.
  6. Rate the geometry. Tube side: Dittus–Boelter, Sieder–Tate or Gnielinski correlations. Shell side: the Kern method for a first pass, then Bell–Delaware, which corrects an ideal tube-bank coefficient for baffle cut, leakage, bypass, unequal end spacing and laminar flow:
    hs = hideal · Jc · Jl · Jb · Js · Jr(7)
    For a well-proportioned exchanger the product of the J factors is around 0.6, so the shell side delivers roughly 60 % of the ideal cross-flow coefficient.
  7. Check pressure drop on both sides against the allowable, and the tube-side velocity against erosion and fouling limits.
  8. Check flow-induced vibration (TEMA has a dedicated section), especially on the shell side of gas and vapour services and at the inlet nozzle.
  9. Hand over to mechanical design: design pressure and temperature for each side, corrosion allowance, materials, the tube-rupture case, and the code (ASME VIII, plus TEMA class and any API supplement).

Report the result as clean U, design (fouled) U and the margin between them. A large gap between the two means the exchanger is mostly fouling allowance, which is worth questioning (see the practical rules below).

Kern against Bell–Delaware. In a textbook comparison for a water service, Kern predicted a shell-side coefficient of 3369 W/m²·K and a pressure drop of 16.4 kPa, while Bell–Delaware gave 3080 W/m²·K and 4.1 kPa, close to a commercial program. Kern is fine for screening heat transfer and poor for shell-side pressure drop.
Tube gauge. 14 BWG on 19.05 mm OD gives a 2.11 mm wall and a 14.83 mm inside diameter, the tube used in the examples below.

    Worked examples

    All numbers here are illustrative and chosen to show the method. Real projects take properties from a simulator and fouling resistances from plant history or the owner's specification.

    Example 1

    Which resistance controls U?

    A light hydrocarbon on the shell side is cooled by cooling water in the tubes. Carbon-steel tubes, 19.05 mm OD, 14 BWG (Di = 14.83 mm), kw = 45 W/m·K. Film coefficients ho = 800 W/m²·K (shell) and hi = 5000 W/m²·K (tube, inside area). Fouling Rf,o = 0.000352 and Rf,i = 0.000176 m²·K/W.

    Apply equation (5) term by term, all referred to the outside area:

    ResistanceValue, m²·K/WShare of total
    Shell-side film, 1/ho0.00125058.5 %
    Shell-side fouling, Rf,o0.00035216.5 %
    Tube-side film, Do/(Dihi)0.00025712.0 %
    Tube-side fouling, Rf,iDo/Di0.00022610.6 %
    Wall, Doln(Do/Di)/2kw0.0000532.5 %
    Total, 1/Uo0.002138100 %
    Uo (fouled) = 468 W/m²·K; Uo (clean) = 641 W/m²·K. The shell-side film is almost 60 % of the resistance, so effort belongs there: closer baffle spacing, a different baffle cut, or low-fin tubes. Doubling the tube-side coefficient would raise U by only about 6 %. The fouling allowances add 37 % to the area, which is a real cost and should be justified.

    Example 2

    Sizing a hydrocarbon cooler and checking F

    20 kg/s of hydrocarbon (cp = 2.40 kJ/kg·K) is cooled from 120 °C to 50 °C with cooling water from 25 °C to 40 °C (cp = 4.18 kJ/kg·K). Use U = 450 W/m²·K, rounded down from Example 1.
    1. Q = 20 × 2.40 × (120 − 50) = 3360 kW
    2. ṁwater = 3360 / (4.18 × 15) = 53.6 kg/s
    3. ΔTa = 120 − 40 = 80 °C; ΔTb = 50 − 25 = 25 °C; ΔTlm = 55 / ln(3.2) = 47.3 °C
    4. P = 15 / 95 = 0.158; R = 70 / 15 = 4.67
    5. F (one 1–2 shell) = 0.909; F (two shells) = 0.980
    6. A = 3 360 000 / (450 × 0.909 × 47.3) = 174 m² → about 476 tubes, 19.05 mm × 6.1 m
    One shell is acceptable here: F = 0.91, area 174 m². A second shell would save only 7 % of the area and add a shell, so it is not worth it. Check the water velocity: with 476 tubes in 2 passes the velocity is about 1.3 m/s, slightly below the 1.5–2.5 m/s usually targeted for cooling water; 4 passes gives about 2.6 m/s. A designer might pick 4 passes and check that the extra pressure drop is allowed, or fewer, longer tubes.

    Example 3

    A steam heater, where F does not matter

    Heat 15 kg/s of water from 20 °C to 80 °C with saturated steam at 5 bar(a) (Tsat = 151.8 °C, hfg = 2108 kJ/kg) condensing on the shell side. Assume U = 2000 W/m²·K.
    1. Q = 15 × 4.18 × 60 = 3762 kW
    2. ṁsteam = 3762 / 2108 = 1.78 kg/s (6420 kg/h)
    3. ΔTa = 151.8 − 80 = 71.8 °C; ΔTb = 151.8 − 20 = 131.8 °C; ΔTlm = 98.8 °C
    4. R = 0, so F = 1 for any number of passes
    5. A = 3 762 000 / (2000 × 98.8) = 19.0 m²
    About 19 m², with no F penalty. The isothermal hot side makes the flow arrangement irrelevant to ΔT. What matters instead is draining condensate (a flooded bundle loses area), venting non-condensables, and controlling the steam side; check whether a control valve on the steam inlet can pull the shell below atmospheric pressure at low load and stall condensate drainage.

    Example 4

    The lean/rich amine cross-exchanger in a CO₂ capture plant

    Hot lean amine leaves the stripper at 120 °C; cold rich amine leaves the absorber at 45 °C. Both streams have a heat-capacity rate of about 380 kW/K (for example 100 kg/s at cp = 3.8 kJ/kg·K), so the exchanger is balanced (R = 1). Compare a minimum approach ΔTmin of 10 °C and 5 °C. Plate exchanger with U ≈ 2400 W/m²·K.

    With balanced flows in true counter-current, the temperature difference is the same at every point and equals ΔTmin, so ΔTlm = ΔTmin and the area follows directly:

    CaseRich out, °CDuty, MWArea, m²P
    ΔTmin = 10 °C11024.710300.867
    ΔTmin = 5 °C11526.622200.933
    Halving the approach recovers 1.9 MW more heat but needs 2.2 times the area. That 1.9 MW would otherwise come from reboiler steam, every hour of operation. At P = 0.87 and R = 1 a single 1–2 shell is thermodynamically impossible; even six shells in series only reach F ≈ 0.76 at a 10 °C approach, and a 5 °C approach needs at least ten shells. This is why plate exchangers, with near-true counter-current flow, dominate this service. Published cost studies on amine plants found the lowest capture cost with gasketed plate exchangers at approaches of about 4–7 °C.

    Example 5

    Does the tube-rupture case need a relief valve?

    A gas cooler has high-pressure gas in the tubes (design 45 barg) and cooling water on the shell (design 10 barg). The exchanger is built to ASME VIII Division 1, hydrotested at 1.3 × design pressure.
    1. API 521 treats tube rupture as unlikely to burst the low-pressure side if that side's test pressure is at least the high-pressure side's design pressure.
    2. With a 1.3 test factor: PLP,design ≥ (10/13) × PHP,design = (10/13) × 45 = 34.6 barg
    3. Actual shell design: 10 barg, below 34.6 barg
    Tube rupture is a credible case. The options are to raise the shell side (and the connected cooling-water piping up to a suitable break) to about 35 barg, or to size relief on the shell side for gas flowing through the two open ends of a guillotine-broken tube, often with a dynamic study, because a gas-into-liquid rupture creates a fast pressure spike that a relief valve may not catch. Meeting the 10/13 criterion does not stop high-pressure fluid from entering the low-pressure system; it only makes loss of containment unlikely, so downstream equipment still needs checking.
    Where the 10/13 comes from. It is the inverse of the 1.3 hydrotest factor in current ASME VIII Division 1. Older vessels tested at 1.5 × design used a 2/3 rule. Check the test pressure actually stamped on the exchanger.
    Steam tables. Saturation values at 5 bar(a) are rounded; take exact values from IAPWS-IF97 for design.
    Amine U value. Industry modelling of plate-and-frame lean/rich exchangers has reported about 425 Btu/h·ft²·°F, roughly 2400 W/m²·K, the value used in Example 4.

    P&ID and control system

    The process datasheet sizes the exchanger; the P&ID decides how it is run, protected and maintained. Figure 4 is the cooler from Example 2 as it would appear on a P&ID: the unit upstream sets the flow, a temperature loop on the exchanger sets the outlet temperature, and a drum downstream takes the product on level control. The symbol names and every instrument tag are listed below the drawing.

    Symbols. Instrument bubbles and letter codes follow ISA 5.1. Many owners have their own legend sheet; it always overrides a generic one.

    Why the control system looks like this

    • Control the outlet with a process bypass, not by throttling the water. Pinching the cooling-water valve is cheaper, but it lowers the tube velocity and raises the return temperature, which are the two conditions that grow scale and silt. The bypass keeps water at full flow and responds fast, because the valve moves the outlet temperature directly instead of waiting for the exchanger's thermal lag.
    • The bypass only works if the exchanger leg has more pressure drop than the open valve. If it does not, add a manual balancing valve in the exchanger leg, or use a three-way valve.
    • Measure after the mixing point. TT-101 sits a few pipe diameters downstream of the tee so the stream is mixed. A thermowell right at the tee reads whichever stream hugs it.
    • Choose fail positions from the hazard. TV-101 fails closed, so an air failure gives full cooling. If over-cooling were the hazard (a waxy or freezing product), it would fail open instead.
    • Upstream flow is a disturbance. When FIC-100 changes throughput, TIC-101 sees it only after the outlet temperature moves. Where the swings are large, a feedforward signal from FT-100 to TIC-101 corrects the bypass before the temperature drifts.
    • Protect the side that can be blocked in. Closing both cooling-water valves while hot oil flows heats trapped water, so the tube side needs thermal relief. In this layout PSV-101 is also the tube-rupture device when the process side is far above the water-side design pressure.
    • Give operators what they need to judge fouling. Inlet and outlet temperature and pressure on both sides, plus block valves and a bypass, let the unit run while the bundle is pulled for cleaning.

    Codes and standards

    Three layers of documents apply to most process exchangers. A pressure code (ASME VIII, EN 13445) makes the vessel legal and safe to operate. A manufacturer standard (TEMA, HEI) fixes construction details, tolerances and nomenclature. An owner's specification (API, ISO, PIP, IPS or a company standard) adds the oil-and-gas lessons learned on top. Process engineers rarely design to these clauses, but they write the datasheets that select them, so they need to know what each one controls.

    Practical rules for students and engineers

    Don't stack your margins

    Fouling resistance, a conservative U and a percentage overdesign are three margins on the same uncertainty. Stacked, they give an exchanger with low velocities, which fouls faster and proves the margins were needed. One practical guide suggests not exceeding about 0.00035 m²·K/W of total fouling resistance unless plant history shows it is needed. Pick one margin, state it, and stick to it.

    Respect the F curve

    F below about 0.8 in a 1–2 shell is a warning, not a number to divide by. It usually means a temperature cross is close. Add shells in series, use a true counter-current type, or relax a terminal temperature.

    Velocity is a design variable

    Cooling water on the tube side is typically kept at 1.5–2.5 m/s, with 0.5–1.0 bar allowed for pressure drop. Below the range it fouls and silts; above it, tubes and inlets erode. Example 2 shows that the choice of tube passes, not just area, settles this.

    Think about thermal expansion early

    A fixed-tubesheet exchanger is the cheapest until the shell and tubes run at very different temperatures, or the start-up case does. Give the mechanical engineer the metal temperatures for start-up, shutdown, steam-out and upset, not only the design point.

    Write the tube-rupture case on the datasheet

    When the two sides have very different design pressures, decide at the process stage whether you will meet the 10/13 rule or provide relief. Raising a design pressure later is expensive.

    Check vibration on the shell side

    Gas and vapour on the shell side, long unsupported spans and high nozzle velocities cause tube failures by vibration. Ask for the vibration analysis with the thermal rating.

    Use software, but check it by hand

    HTRI and Aspen EDR model leakage and bypass streams far better than any hand method. The hand checks on this page catch the common input errors: wrong phase, wrong units on fouling, the wrong side for a fluid, or a duty that does not match the energy balance.

    In Canada, plan for registration

    An exchanger built and stamped to ASME still needs a CRN for the province where it is installed. Ask the vendor whether the design is already registered in your province.

    A habit worth forming. Write down which resistance controls U before you change anything. Most improvements that do not touch the controlling resistance do nothing.
    Datasheets to learn. The TEMA specification sheet (free from TEMA in SI and US units), the API 660 and API 661 data sheets, and the PIP electronic data sheets.
