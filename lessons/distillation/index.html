Lessons / Separation

Distillation columns, with and without reflux or reboiler

How a column separates by stages, when you can leave out the condenser or the reboiler, and how to design, control and protect one, with an interactive P&ID, ten worked examples and twenty problems to solve.

Alireza Moghaddam, EIT Updated 15 November 2026 About 2 hours read, longer with the problems SI units; model curves and vendor-style sheets are illustrative

Distillation is the workhorse separation of the process industries, and also one of its largest users of energy. A good column is a quiet one; a poor one shows up as off-spec product, a flooded tower, a lifting relief valve or, at worst, a release.

I wrote this page for chemical and process engineers who size columns, check vendor proposals and sit in HAZOPs, and for students who want to see how the textbook methods connect to real equipment. Figure 1 is the whole idea in one place: change the configuration and watch which operating line disappears. A stripper has no reflux, so it has no rectifying section and can only clean up the bottoms. A rectifier has no reboiler, so it can only purify the distillate. Live steam replaces the reboiler but dilutes the bottoms.

The sections below build up every step behind that figure, then go on to multicomponent shortcuts, trays and packings, hydraulics and sizing, vendor data, standards and safety. If you can solve the problems at the end without opening the solutions, you can carry a binary or simple multicomponent column from a process specification to a vendor enquiry, and you will know which questions to ask in the HAZOP.

01

What a distillation column does

Distillation separates a liquid mixture by exploiting a difference in volatility. When a liquid boils, the vapour it makes is richer in the more volatile (light) component than the liquid it came from. One boil and condense is a flash, and it rarely gives a pure product. A column repeats that step many times in one shell: vapour rises, liquid falls, and on every tray or every short height of packing the two phases meet, exchange material and leave roughly in equilibrium.

Heat goes in at the bottom and comes out at the top. The reboiler vaporises part of the bottom liquid and sends it up as boil-up. The condenser liquefies the top vapour; part is returned as reflux and the rest is drawn off as distillate. Reflux provides the falling liquid above the feed, and boil-up provides the rising vapour below it. The section above the feed enriches the vapour in the light component (the rectifying or enriching section); the section below strips the light component out of the liquid (the stripping section).

Every column design is a trade between two things you pay for. More reflux (more energy) means fewer stages (a shorter column); fewer stages need more reflux. At the two limits, minimum reflux needs an infinite number of stages and total reflux needs the minimum number of stages but makes no product. Real designs sit between, usually at 1.1 to 1.5 times the minimum reflux 2.

The single most useful number is the relative volatility, α. It tells you at a glance how hard a split is. Above about 2 the column is short and cheap; below about 1.2 it becomes a superfractionator with well over a hundred trays, like the propylene–propane splitter 23.

α = (y₁ / x₁) / (y₂ / x₂) = K₁ / K₂(1)
y* = α x / (1 + (α − 1) x)(2)

x and y are mole fractions of the light component in the liquid and the vapour; K = y/x is the equilibrium ratio; equation (2) is the equilibrium curve for constant α.

Theoretical stage. A contact that sends vapour and liquid out in equilibrium with each other. Real trays get part of the way; the ratio is the tray efficiency.

Light and heavy keys. In a multicomponent feed, the two components whose split you specify: the light key mostly goes up, the heavy key mostly goes down.

Reflux ratio. R = L/D, the reflux returned to the column per unit of distillate.

02

With and without reflux or reboiler

A full column has both a condenser with reflux and a reboiler, so it can make two pure products. Take one of them away and you lose one section’s ability to purify. That is often exactly what you want: a cheaper column for a job that only needs one clean product.

The stripper: what you give up without reflux

In a stripping column the liquid feed itself is the only liquid in the column. The operating line runs from the bottoms composition, (xB, xB), with slope L̄/V̄ greater than 1, and ends at the feed composition. The vapour leaving the top tray is the overhead product, so it can never be richer than the vapour that would be in equilibrium with the feed. More boil-up gives cleaner bottoms but a leaner, larger overhead. The minimum boil-up is set by a pinch at the top of the column (Example 4).

The rectifier: what you give up without a reboiler

In a rectifying column the vapour feed is the only vapour. The operating line runs from (xD, xD) with slope L/V below 1, and the bottom liquid composition falls out of the balance. Pinch is at the bottom: the bottom liquid can approach, but not pass, equilibrium with the feed vapour. More reflux gives a richer distillate but sends more of the light component down with the bottoms (Example 5).

Live steam instead of a reboiler

When the heavy component is water, steam can be blown straight into the bottom of the column. You save the reboiler, its fouling and its corrosion, and you can strip at a lower bottom temperature. The cost is dilution: every kilogram of steam condensed ends up in the bottoms, so for the same bottoms mole fraction more of the light component is lost. On the McCabe–Thiele diagram the stripping line now ends at (xB, 0) instead of on the diagonal, because the vapour entering the bottom tray contains no light component. The overall balance gains a stream: F + S = D + B (Example 6).

Rule of thumb. If only one product needs to be pure, ask whether a stripper or a rectifier will do before buying a second exchanger and its controls.

Batch distillation is a rectifier on top of a still pot that acts as the reboiler. The pot composition falls with time, so reflux is raised or the cut is changed as the run goes on.

03

Types of columns and where they are used

Columns are classified in several overlapping ways: by operation (continuous or batch), by internals (trays or packing), by pressure (vacuum, atmospheric or pressure), and by how the separation is achieved when plain distillation is not enough.

Continuous columns

Steady feed and products; by far the most common. Design methods on this page assume steady state.

Refineries, gas plants, petrochemicals, bulk chemicals.

Batch columns

A still pot, a rectifying column and a condenser. Flexible for many products and small volumes; compositions change with time.

Pharmaceuticals, fine chemicals, solvent recovery, spirits.

Trayed columns

Discrete stages; easy to inspect and clean, tolerant of high liquid rates and fouling, suited to large diameters and side draws.

Most pressure columns, fouling services, amine and sour-water units.

Packed columns

Random or structured packing; low pressure drop per stage, small hold-up. Usually the first choice under vacuum and in small diameters 33.

Vacuum and heat-sensitive service, revamps for capacity, corrosive service in plastics or ceramics.

Vacuum columns

Lower pressure lowers boiling points, which protects heat-sensitive products. Structured packing keeps the pressure drop, and hence the bottom temperature, low; it is used in columns making about 70 % of the world’s styrene monomer 36.

Crude vacuum units, styrene, fatty acids, fragrances.

Superfractionators

Close-boiling splits with α near 1. The propylene–propane splitter typically has 150 to 250 trays and a reflux-to-feed ratio of 5 to 10, and is often heat-pumped 23.

C₃ and C₂ splitters, xylene isomers.

Crude and multi-draw fractionators

One feed, many products drawn from the side, with pumparounds to remove heat and steam-stripped side strippers. The main column has no reboiler: heat comes from the fired heater and stripping steam.

Atmospheric and vacuum crude units, FCC main fractionators.

Extractive distillation

A heavy solvent added near the top changes the relative volatility of the keys; the solvent leaves with the bottoms and is recovered in a second column.

Aromatics recovery, ethanol dehydration with glycols, concentrating hydrochloric acid with sulfuric acid 22.

Azeotropic and pressure-swing distillation

An entrainer forms a new, lower-boiling azeotrope that is decanted; or two columns at different pressures exploit an azeotrope that moves with pressure (section 05).

Ethanol and isopropanol dehydration, acetic acid, HCl–water.

Reactive distillation

Reaction and separation in one shell; removing a product drives an equilibrium reaction forward.

Ethers such as MTBE, esters such as methyl acetate.

Dividing-wall columns

A vertical wall turns one shell into a thermally coupled (Petlyuk) arrangement that makes three products with one reboiler and one condenser. Koch-Glitsch puts the potential savings at 20–30 % of capital and 20–50 % of energy against two conventional columns 24.

Chemicals (BASF built the first in 1985; hundreds now run), reformate splitting, NGL fractionation 24.

Heat-pumped columns

The overhead vapour is compressed and condensed in the reboiler, so the condenser duty becomes the reboiler duty. Works best when the top-to-bottom temperature difference is small 23.

C₃ splitters, close-boiling alcohols.

04

Vapour–liquid equilibrium curves

Everything in a column design rests on the vapour–liquid equilibrium (VLE). If the equilibrium data are wrong near the product purity, no amount of careful hydraulics will rescue the design. At low to moderate pressure the modified Raoult’s law is the working relation:

yi P = xi γi Pisat(T)(3)
ln Psat [kPa] = A − B / (T [°C] + C)(4)

γi is the liquid activity coefficient (1 for an ideal solution); equation (4) is the Antoine equation. Constants for common solvents are tabulated in thermodynamics texts 5.

Two calculations come up again and again. A bubble point finds the temperature at which a liquid of known composition starts to boil, and the composition of that first bubble: solve Σ xi γi Pisat(T) = P for T. A dew point does the same for a vapour starting to condense: solve Σ yi P / (γi Pisat) = 1. The top tray of a column with a total condenser sits at the distillate’s bubble point; the reboiler sits at the bottoms’ bubble point.

Reading the two diagrams

The T–x–y diagram (Figure 3, left) has two curves at a fixed pressure. The lower one is the bubble-point curve, the upper one the dew-point curve. Below the bubble curve the mixture is all liquid, above the dew curve all vapour, and in between it splits into two phases. A horizontal tie line at any temperature joins a liquid composition x to the vapour composition y in equilibrium with it; the lever rule along that line gives the vapour fraction.

The x–y diagram (Figure 3, right) plots y against x along with the 45° line. The further the curve bows away from the diagonal, the easier the split. This is the diagram McCabe–Thiele uses. Where it crosses the diagonal there is an azeotrope.

Where to get VLE. Measured data (the DECHEMA collection, the NIST ThermoData Engine) first; then a fitted activity model (Wilson, NRTL, UNIQUAC) for polar liquids, or an equation of state (Peng–Robinson, SRK) for hydrocarbons and gases. Check any model against data at the product purities.

Pressure and α. For most mixtures α rises as pressure falls, which is one reason C₃ splitters are run at the lowest pressure the heat sink allows 23.

05

Azeotropes and how to get past them

An azeotrope is a mixture whose vapour has the same composition as its liquid. At that point y = x, α = 1, and boiling changes nothing, so ordinary distillation cannot cross it. On the McCabe–Thiele diagram the equilibrium curve meets the diagonal, and no reflux ratio can step past the meeting point. Try the “Ethanol near the azeotrope” preset in Figure 1 and push xD towards 0.92.

  • Minimum-boiling (positive deviation, γ > 1). Unlike molecules repel each other, the mixture is more volatile than either component, and the azeotrope boils below both pure components. Ethanol–water at 101.3 kPa boils at 78.15 °C at about 95.6 wt % ethanol (4.4 wt % water), just below ethanol’s 78.3 °C 19. Most azeotropes are of this kind.
  • Maximum-boiling (negative deviation, γ < 1). Unlike molecules attract; the azeotrope boils above both components. Acetone–chloroform boils at 64.5 °C at an acetone mole fraction of about 0.34 21; hydrochloric acid has a constant-boiling mixture at 20.2 wt % HCl and 108.6 °C 22.
  • Heterogeneous azeotropes. The liquid splits into two phases at the azeotrope. Benzene–water boils at 69.3 °C, well below either component, and the condensate separates into a benzene-rich and a water-rich layer 20. The two layers are the basis of decanter-assisted azeotropic distillation.

How pressure moves an azeotrope

Azeotropic composition changes with pressure. For ethanol–water the water content rises from 4.4 wt % at 760 mmHg to 4.75 wt % at 1,451 mmHg, and the azeotrope disappears below roughly 75 mmHg 19. That movement is small, which is why ethanol is not dried by pressure swing. Where an azeotrope moves a lot, two columns at different pressures can walk around it: one column makes pure A and an azeotrope at pressure p₁, the other takes that azeotrope at p₂, where it has a different composition, and makes pure B. HCl–water can be concentrated this way 22.

For three or more components with azeotropes, the tool is the residue curve map: the paths a liquid composition follows during simple boiling. Distillation boundaries on that map cannot be crossed by a single column, so the map tells you quickly which product sequences are possible before any simulation is run.

Converting units. 95.6 wt % ethanol is (95.6/46.07) / (95.6/46.07 + 4.4/18.02) = 0.895 mole fraction. Keep mass and mole bases straight: VLE diagrams are almost always in mole fraction.

Fuel ethanol. Plants typically take the beer-column vapour to a rectifier close to the azeotrope and finish the drying on molecular sieves, where pressure-swing adsorption takes over from distillation.

06

Mass and energy balances

Before any stage is stepped off, close the balances. They fix the product flows, the internal liquid and vapour traffic (which sizes the diameter) and the condenser and reboiler duties (which size the exchangers and the utilities).

Material balance

F = D + B(5)
F zF = D xD + B xB → D = F (zF − xB) / (xD − xB)(6)

The recovery of the light component in the distillate is D xD / (F zF). For a multicomponent feed write equation (6) for every component and specify the split with two numbers, usually the recovery of the light key in the distillate and of the heavy key in the bottoms.

Feed condition, q

The feed’s thermal state decides how it splits between the two sections. q is the fraction of the feed that adds to the liquid flowing down below the feed tray:

q = (HV − HF) / (HV − HL) ≈ 1 + cp,L (Tbubble − TF) / λ (subcooled)(7)
q-line: y = [q / (q − 1)] x − zF / (q − 1)(8)
Feed conditionqSlope of the q-line
Subcooled liquid> 1Positive, steeper than the diagonal
Saturated liquid (at its bubble point)1Vertical, x = zF

Saturated vapour (at its dew point)0Horizontal, y = zF
Superheated vapour< 0Positive, shallower than the diagonal

Section flows

With constant molal overflow (equal molar latent heats, negligible heat of mixing and heat loss), liquid and vapour flows are constant within each section:

L = R D V = (R + 1) D L̄ = L + q F V̄ = V − (1 − q) F(9)

Energy balance

F hF + QR = D hD + B hB + QC(10)
QC ≈ V λtop QR ≈ V̄ λbottom(11)

Equation (11) holds for a total condenser with saturated reflux and saturated products. Subcooled reflux adds to QC and to the internal reflux.

Equation (11) is why distillation is energy-hungry: every mole of distillate is boiled R + 1 times. Cutting R from 2.0 to 1.5 cuts the reboiler duty by about a sixth. It is also why the column, the condenser and the reboiler must be designed together: the diameter follows V and V̄, and so do the exchangers.

Live steam

With open steam S (saturated, constant molal overflow) the stripping section has V̄ = S and L̄ = B, and the overall balances become:

F + S = D + B F zF = D xD + B xB(12)

Latent heats at the normal boiling point. Benzene 30.7 kJ/mol, toluene 33.2 kJ/mol, water 40.7 kJ/mol, methanol 35.2 kJ/mol. Equal molar latent heats are what make constant molal overflow a fair assumption for many organic pairs.

When CMO fails. Large differences in molar latent heat or strong heats of mixing (ammonia–water, for example) need an enthalpy–composition (Ponchon–Savarit) method or a rigorous simulation.

07

The McCabe–Thiele method

McCabe–Thiele is a graphical solution of the stage-by-stage equilibrium and balance equations for a binary mixture with constant molal overflow. Simulators replaced it for design long ago, but it remains the best way to see what a column is doing, why it pinches and what reflux buys you. Figure 1 draws it for you; here is how to draw it by hand.

  1. Draw the equilibrium curve and the 45° line on an x–y diagram.
  2. Mark xB, zF and xD on the diagonal.
  3. Draw the q-line from (zF, zF) with slope q/(q − 1).
  4. Draw the rectifying line from (xD, xD) with slope L/V = R/(R + 1).
  5. Draw the stripping line from (xB, xB) to the point where the rectifying line meets the q-line.
  6. Starting at (xD, xD), step horizontally to the equilibrium curve (one equilibrium stage), then vertically to the operating line (the balance between stages). Switch to the stripping line on the first step that passes the intersection: that stage is the optimum feed stage.
  7. Stop when x ≤ xB. With a partial reboiler the last step is the reboiler; with a partial condenser the first step is the condenser.
Rectifying line: y = [R / (R + 1)] x + xD / (R + 1)(13)
Stripping line: y = (L̄ / V̄) x − (B / V̄) xB(14)

The two limits

Minimum reflux. Lower R and the rectifying line swings down towards the equilibrium curve. When the operating lines first touch the curve, a pinch forms: the steps get infinitely small and the number of stages goes to infinity. For a well-behaved curve the pinch is at the feed; for curves with an inflection, such as ethanol–water, it can be a tangent pinch above the feed. For constant α and a saturated-liquid feed, Underwood’s equation gives Rmin directly:

Rmin = [xD / zF − α (1 − xD) / (1 − zF)] / (α − 1)(15)

Total reflux. With no product withdrawn, both operating lines fall on the diagonal and the number of stages is the minimum. For constant α the Fenske equation gives it (stages include the reboiler):

Nmin = ln{ [xD / (1 − xD)] · [(1 − xB) / xB] } / ln α(16)

Operating lines when a section is missing

Stripper (no reflux): y = s x − (s − 1) xB, s = L̄/V̄ = F/V̄, top point (zF, yD)(17)
Rectifier (no reboiler): y = s x + (1 − s) xD, s = R/(R+1), xB = [zF − (1 − s) xD] / s(18)
Live steam: y = (B / S) (x − xB), which passes through (xB, 0)(19)

Practical points

  • At high purity the steps near the ends become tiny. Redraw the ends on log scales, or use the Fenske or Kremser equations for that part of the column.
  • A partial condenser is an equilibrium stage; a total condenser is not.
  • Each side draw or second feed adds a new operating-line segment with its own slope.
  • The method assumes constant pressure. A column with a large pressure drop (vacuum columns in particular) has a different equilibrium curve at the top and the bottom.

Try it. In Figure 1, set the multiple of minimum reflux to 1.05 and watch the steps crowd around the feed: that is the pinch. Then set it to 3 and see how few stages are left.

Feed on the wrong tray costs stages: the steps get smaller on one side. A real column usually has two or three alternative feed nozzles for that reason.

08

Shortcut methods for multicomponent columns

For multicomponent feeds the McCabe–Thiele picture no longer applies, but four classic equations give a quick design that is good enough to start a rigorous simulation and to check its answers 2. Pick the light key (LK) and the heavy key (HK), specify their recoveries, and use α relative to the heavy key at an average column temperature.

Fenske: minimum stages and the non-key split

Nmin = ln[ (dLK / bLK) (bHK / dHK) ] / ln αLK,HK(20)
di / bi = (dHK / bHK) · αi,HKNmin(21)

Underwood: minimum reflux

Σ αi zi / (αi − θ) = 1 − q, with αHK < θ < αLK(22)
Rmin + 1 = Σ αi xD,i / (αi − θ)(23)

Gilliland: stages at a real reflux ratio

Gilliland’s correlation links the two limits. Molokanov’s fit of it is convenient for calculation:

Y = 1 − exp{ [(1 + 54.4 X) / (11 + 117.2 X)] · [(X − 1) / √X] }(24)

X = (R − Rmin)/(R + 1) and Y = (N − Nmin)/(N + 1); N includes the reboiler.

Kirkbride: where to put the feed

NR / NS = [ (B/D) (zHK / zLK) (xLK,B / xHK,D)² ]0.206(25)

Example 3 runs all four on a four-component feed. Figure 4 shows how well Gilliland’s correlation reproduces the stage counts from Figure 1 for a constant-α binary: the points stay close to the curve across the whole range of reflux.

When the shortcuts mislead. Strongly non-ideal mixtures, azeotropes, large changes in α along the column, and wide-boiling feeds with large flow changes between sections. Use them to initialise a rigorous model (Aspen Plus RadFrac, HYSYS), never to replace it.

Distributed non-keys. If a non-key has α between the keys or close to them, it splits between the products; equation (21) tells you how much.

09

From theoretical stages to real trays and packing

Real trays do not reach equilibrium, and packing does not come in stages. Two numbers convert the theoretical answer into hardware: the overall tray efficiency for trays and the height equivalent to a theoretical plate (HETP) for packing.

Eo = Ntheoretical / Nactual EMV = (yn − yn+1) / (y*n − yn+1)(26)

Eo is the overall (section) efficiency; EMV is the Murphree vapour efficiency of a single tray.

For a first estimate, O’Connell’s 1946 correlation is still widely used. It needs only the relative volatility of the keys and the liquid viscosity in mPa·s, both at the average column temperature 9:

Eo = 0.492 (α μL)−0.245(27)

Kister puts its accuracy at about ±25 % 6, and its predictions tend to be conservative. Viscous liquids and high α both lower the efficiency. Field experience is the better guide when you have it:

Packed height

Z = Ntheoretical × HETP(28)

Kister’s rules of thumb for HETP in metres, for organic and hydrocarbon systems with surface tension below 25 mN/m and good industrial distribution, accurate to about ±25 % 6:

Metal random packings, 25 mm and larger: HETP = 93 / ap(29)

Vendors publish their own HETP curves and correlations, usually against the F-factor at several pressures; section 12 shows how to read them. Whatever the source, most designers add a margin to the stage count or the packed height, and the size of that margin should reflect how good the VLE data are.

Why absorbers are less efficient. In an absorber the liquid-side resistance dominates and the stripping factor is far from 1, so each tray achieves a smaller fraction of equilibrium.

Bed height. Liquid maldistribution grows with depth, so tall packed sections are split into beds with redistributors between them.

10

Trays, packings and other internals

A cross-flow tray is a perforated deck with a weir at one end and a downcomer at each end. Liquid comes down the inlet downcomer, flows across the deck as a froth while vapour bubbles up through the openings, spills over the outlet weir and goes down the next downcomer. Figure 5 names the parts that appear on every tray data sheet.

Tray types

  • Sieve trays. A deck punched with holes; a common standard is a 2 mm deck with 12.7 mm holes and 10 % open area 10. Cheapest and easy to clean, with low pressure drop, but they weep first as the vapour rate falls and have the narrowest operating range 11.
  • Moving (floating) valve trays. Developed in the 1950s: each hole has a cap held in a cage or by legs that lifts with the vapour rate and closes when it falls. Higher capacity than bubble caps and better turndown than sieve trays 11. Valves can wear, stick or pop out in fouling or cyclic service.
  • Fixed valve trays. Valves punched directly from the deck, introduced in the early 1990s; more capacity than moving valves, no moving parts, good in mildly fouling service 11.
  • Bubble-cap trays. A riser with a cap over it. Because there is no open hole in the deck, they cannot weep, which keeps them in use for very low liquid rates and wide-turndown services such as glycol contactors 11. Lower capacity and higher pressure drop and cost.
  • Dual-flow trays. No downcomers; liquid and vapour pass counter-currently through the same holes. Good in fouling and slurry service, narrow operating range.
  • Baffle (shed, disc-and-donut) trays. Liquid curtains rather than froth; very fouling-resistant, low efficiency. Used in heavy-oil and slurry sections.
  • High-capacity and high-performance trays. Multiple-downcomer trays, sloped or truncated downcomers, pushing valves that move liquid across the deck, and enhanced inlet areas. Hydraulics: flooding, diameter and height

The stage count fixes the height; the vapour and liquid traffic fixes the diameter. A tray works only inside a window. Push too much vapour and it floods; too much liquid and the downcomers back up or choke; too little vapour and it weeps or dumps. Sizing means placing the design point and the turndown point inside that window with margin.

The limits of a tray

  • Jet (entrainment) flood. Vapour carries so much liquid to the tray above that liquid accumulates and the column fills. The usual upper limit in low- to moderate-pressure columns.
  • Downcomer backup flood. The liquid head needed to push liquid down the downcomer, against the tray pressure drop, rises until the froth in the downcomer reaches the tray above.
  • Downcomer choke. The froth entering the downcomer is too fast to release its vapour. With high-pressure distillation and foaming systems this is the most common capacity limit 6.
  • Weeping and dumping. The lower limit: not enough vapour pressure drop to hold liquid on the deck.
  • Spray regime. At low liquid loads the deck liquid is blown into spray. Weir loads below about 2–3 US gpm per inch of weir (18–27 m³/h per metre) can produce it 6.

Reading packing data

Packing vendors publish three kinds of curve, and each has a basis you must check before using it:

  • Pressure drop against F-factor, one curve per liquid load. Koch-Glitsch’s IMTP curves, for example, are for air and water at ambient conditions in a 0.76 m (30 in) column, at liquid loads up to about 150 m³/(m²·h) 15. Air–water data overstate pressure drop for organics, which wet better and foam less.
  • HETP (or stages per metre) against F-factor, often one curve per pressure. Sulzer’s structured-packing curves are drawn for head pressures from 960 down to 50 mbar 14. HETP is flat over the middle of the range and rises at both ends: at low F because of poor wetting, at high F because of entrainment and approach to flood.
  • Capacity, as a maximum F-factor or a maximum efficient capacity. Sulzer quotes about 0.3–1.0 mbar per theoretical stage for Mellapak 250.Y, and 0.1–0.5 mbar per stage for BX gauze, which is why gauze is used for deep vacuum 14.

To use a curve, compute your superficial F-factor (F = us√ρV), read the HETP at your pressure, correct it for your surface tension and viscosity if the vendor gives a correlation (Koch-Glitsch’s IMTP HETP correlation includes both 15), check the pressure drop per metre, and confirm the design F-factor is well below the maximum efficient capacity at both maximum and minimum rates.

Questions to ask every vendor

  • What test system, pressure and column diameter is the curve or correlation based on? FRI data or the vendor’s own rig?
  • Which flood definition and which system factor did you use?
  • What is guaranteed: capacity, pressure drop, efficiency, or only the hydraulic rating?
  • What is the turndown at the minimum case, and what limits it?
  • For packing: distributor type, drip-point density, levelling tolerance and the expected HETP with that distributor.
  • What installation and inspection tolerances apply, and who checks them?

Reflux, energy and pressure

The reboiler duty grows roughly in proportion to R + 1, while the number of stages falls steeply as R rises from the minimum and then flattens (Figures 1 and 4). The total annual cost therefore has a shallow minimum, usually somewhere between 1.1 and 1.5 times the minimum reflux 2. When energy is expensive, the optimum moves towards the low end and the column gets taller.

Choosing the pressure

  • Start from the condenser. Choose the lowest pressure at which the overhead can be condensed against the cheapest heat sink (cooling water or air) with a reasonable approach; refrigeration is expensive.
  • Check the bottom. A higher pressure raises the bottom temperature; heat-sensitive or polymerising products set an upper limit, which may force vacuum.
  • Check α. Most mixtures separate more easily at lower pressure, so the pressure choice trades condenser cost against column size and reboiler duty.
  • Check the other side of every exchanger: the available steam or hot-oil temperature for the reboiler, and whether the condenser can heat another stream.

Saving energy

  • Feed preheat with the hot bottoms; a partly vaporised feed shifts duty from the reboiler to the feed preheater.
  • Side reboilers and side condensers (pumparounds) move heat at less extreme temperatures, as crude columns do.
  • Heat pumping for close boilers: compress the overhead and condense it in the reboiler 23.
  • Thermal coupling: dividing-wall columns for three-product splits 24.
  • Better internals: more stages in the same shell lower the reflux needed for the same split.
  • Tight control: over-purifying a product wastes energy; controlling closer to specification saves it.

Scale of the prize. Koch-Glitsch puts distillation at nearly 40 % of energy use in refining and chemical manufacturing 11. A few percent off the reflux of a large column is worth real money and real emissions.

Control system, P&ID and troubleshooting

A two-product column with a total condenser has five valves that matter: reflux, distillate, bottoms, reboiler heat and condenser duty. The feed is usually set by the unit upstream. Three of the five must hold inventory, because column pressure, the reflux-drum level and the sump level do not regulate themselves. That leaves two for the two product compositions 38. Most columns close one of those two with a tray temperature and leave the other on flow or ratio control 38.

Figure 8 draws that on a piping and instrumentation diagram (P&ID), the way a project would: every loop, each valve and how it fails, the alarms, and a separate layer of trips and relief.

Reading the symbols

Instrument tags follow ISA-5.1. The first letter is the measured variable: F flow, L level, P pressure, T temperature, A analysis, PD differential pressure. The letters after it say what the device does: T transmit, I indicate, C control, V valve, AHH alarm high-high 45. All parts of one loop share its number, so FT-104 measures, FIC-104 controls and FV-104 moves.

A plain circle is a field instrument. A circle in a square is a function in the basic process control system (BPCS). A diamond in a square is a safety instrumented system (SIS) function, the meaning ISA-5.1 now gives that symbol 44. A line across the middle means the operator can reach it in the control room 45. Dashed lines are electric signals. A line of small circles is a software link inside the control system: here, a cascade from one controller into a flow controller’s set point 45. FC or FO beside a valve says whether it fails closed or open when it loses its air or signal.

Why every valve has a flow loop. Each valve in Figure 8 is moved by a flow controller, even when the real target is a level or a temperature. The flow loop corrects changes in upstream and downstream pressure within seconds, so the outer loop works with a clean, nearly linear handle.

LV or DV: which stream holds the drum level

The level loops decide which flows are left for composition, so they are the first real choice. In the standard LV scheme the distillate D holds the drum level and the bottoms B hold the sump, leaving reflux L and boil-up V for composition. In the DV scheme the reflux holds the drum level and the distillate rate is set directly, so a change in D changes the split at once. DV and LB are called material-balance schemes for that reason 38.

Two rules decide most cases. First, hold a level with the largest stream leaving the vessel; one common guide uses reflux for the drum level when L/D is above roughly 6 38. A column at a reflux ratio of 15 cannot hold its drum level with a distillate valve that handles a sixteenth of the liquid. Second, the level tuning should not change the composition response. That favours LV: with DV, how the column responds to a change in boil-up depends on how the drum level is tuned 38. Skogestad recommends LV with a fast temperature loop for most columns; the temperature loop also gives indirect level control, which makes LV workable for many high-reflux columns 38. Switch Figure 8 between the two schemes and watch the cascades move.

Where the temperature goes

A tray temperature is a cheap, fast stand-in for composition, but only on the right tray. The usual rules 38,39:

  • Put it where the temperature changes most from one tray to the next, and avoid flat parts of the profile. For a binary column the slope of the profile matches the gain of tray temperature to reflux closely 39.
  • Put it inside the column, not at the ends, where temperature hardly moves with composition.
  • Put it at the end whose product matters most, usually the more valuable one.
  • Close a stripping-section temperature with boil-up. Reflux reaches a bottom tray only after a delay, so it should not control a bottom temperature 38.
  • Correct it for pressure. A tray temperature moves with pressure as well as composition, so many systems control a pressure-compensated temperature.

Luyben compared five criteria (slope, sensitivity, singular value decomposition, invariant temperature, and least change in product purity) on columns from ideal binary to azeotropic multicomponent. Singular value decomposition gave the most effective structure 40, but the slope rule is the one most engineers try first. Install spare thermowells on a few trays either side of the chosen one; the best tray in operation is often not the one the simulation picked.

An on-line analyser measures composition directly, but a gas chromatograph takes minutes per sample. Use it to trim the temperature set point slowly, not to move a valve.

The names are confusing. Some texts call LV an energy-balance scheme, because L and V are the energy-related flows, and DV or LB material-balance schemes. Write on the P&ID which flow holds which level and the names stop mattering.

Tune from the inside out. Flow loops first, then pressure, then levels, then the temperature. A tray temperature that moves with pressure will chase a poorly tuned pressure loop.

How the valves fail

Every control valve on the P&ID is given a position to go to when it loses air or signal, chosen so the column heads toward a cool, contained state. As a general rule, heating fails closed and cooling fails open 46. On a column that usually means reboiler steam and feed fail closed and condenser coolant fails open. Product draws fail closed, so the column keeps its inventory and vapour cannot blow through an empty drum or sump into lower-pressure equipment. Reflux is often set to fail open, to keep the top trays wet while the heat input dies away. None of this is automatic: an open reflux valve can flood the top or empty the drum, so each position is confirmed in the HAZOP.

What actually goes wrong

Kister’s survey of 900 published malfunction case histories found plugging (of tray active areas, packing and distributors) at the top of the list, with coking, scale and corrosion, and precipitation as the usual causes. The tower base came second: liquid rising above the reboiler inlet caused premature flooding, internals damage and even tower overfilling 7. Installation mishaps are also among the top five causes 31.

  • Flooding shows as a rising and erratic pressure drop on PDI-107, worse separation, falling bottoms level and carry-over into the reflux drum.
  • Foaming shows as flooding at loads well below the design, often after a contaminant arrives (hydrocarbons in amine, corrosion inhibitors, fines).
  • Weeping and dumping show as a loss of efficiency at low rates, and sometimes as an unstable pressure drop.
  • Damaged internals show as a step change in pressure drop or separation after an upset, a start-up or a pressure surge.
  • A misbehaving control loop often looks like a process problem. Put the loop in manual: if the swings stop, the cause is tuning, a sticking valve or a bad measurement, not the column.

Gamma scans locate flooding, missing trays, foaming and maldistribution without opening the column; temperature surveys and pressure-drop profiles narrow the search. Every opening of the column is a chance to inspect: levelness, weir heights, downcomer clearances, missing clamps and debris.

What governs the shell

For a column at modest pressure, internal pressure rarely sets the wall thickness. Wind and seismic bending on a tall, slender shell, external pressure, and the weight of a column full of water during the hydrotest usually do. The internal-pressure check for a cylindrical shell (ASME VIII-1, UG-27, circumferential stress) is still the first step:

t = P R / (S E − 0.6 P) + c(41)

P design pressure, R inside radius in the corroded condition, S allowable stress at design temperature, E joint efficiency, c corrosion allowance.

  • Vacuum. Any column that is steamed out can be pulled into vacuum when the steam condenses, and many engineers design such columns for full vacuum as a matter of course; the extra cost is often just thicker stiffening rings 30. If the column is not rated for vacuum, protect it with vacuum breakers and procedures, and never block it in after steaming.
  • Hydrostatic head. The bottom of a tall column sees the design pressure plus the liquid head at relief conditions, and the hydrotest adds the full water head.
  • Tray supports and uplift. Trays sit on rings welded to the shell and are clamped down. Specify an uplift rating where pressure surges are possible: water entering a hot column, rapid depressuring, or steam introduced too fast at start-up.
  • Levelness. An out-of-level tray has an uneven liquid depth and loses efficiency and capacity. A typical acceptance limit is 3 mm out-of-level for columns up to 2 m diameter and 6 mm above 31; check the support rings at the fabrication shop, before the trays go in 31.
  • Base and sump. The reboiler return must stay above the highest liquid level, with room for surge; Kister’s survey ranks tower-base problems second only to plugging 7. Fit vortex breakers on bottoms outlets and specify independent level instruments.
  • Skirt and fireproofing. A pool fire under a column can weaken an unprotected skirt and bring the whole tower down; fireproof skirts and supports in fire zones.

Manways. Usually one every 10 to 20 trays, plus at feeds, draws and the bottom; the trays below a manway need removable panels that pass through it.

Materials. Carbon steel with a corrosion allowance for most hydrocarbon shells; stainless or higher alloys for trays and packing, where there is no allowance to spend.

Safety: hazards and safeguards

A column holds a large flammable inventory near its boiling point, with a heat source at the bottom and a heat sink at the top. Most of its serious hazards come from losing the balance between the two, from losing track of where the liquid is, or from what happens when the column is opened.

Overpressure: the scenarios API 521 asks about

API 521 gives no credit for control action that would reduce the load; credit can be taken for a reboiler “pinch” as the column temperature rises at relieving pressure 27. Overfill: what Texas City taught

On 23 March 2005, during the start-up of the isomerisation unit at BP’s Texas City refinery, operators filled the raffinate splitter, a distillation column roughly 50 m tall, for about three hours without taking any liquid out. The level rose to about 15 times its normal value, the contents were overheated, the relief valves opened, and liquid hydrocarbon went to a blowdown drum and stack that vented to atmosphere. The vapour cloud ignited, killing 15 people and injuring 180 25.

The CSB found that the level transmitter and the high-level alarm were not working properly and misled the operators, that start-up procedures were not followed, and that 13 of the previous 16 start-ups had shown excessive levels without being investigated 25. Afterwards BP said it would eliminate blowdown stacks venting directly to atmosphere at its US refineries 25.

  • Measure tower level with two independent instruments, compare them, and alarm on disagreement; make the high-high level trip independent of the control transmitter.
  • Treat start-up and shutdown as the hazardous operations they are: written procedures, experienced supervision, instruments checked before feed is introduced.
  • Relieve flammable materials to a closed flare system, not to an atmospheric vent.
  • Site occupied buildings and trailers out of reach of a credible vapour-cloud explosion (API RP 752 and 753).
  • Investigate near misses such as abnormal levels and pressures; they are the warnings.

Fires inside packed columns

Corrosion of carbon steel by hydrogen sulfide forms iron sulfide, which settles on packing and ignites in air at ambient temperature. Structured packing is especially exposed: it is made of thin sheet (0.1–0.2 mm) with a huge surface, and once lit it burns as a metal fire 26. One operator reported that all of its packed-column fires had occurred in structured beds, none in random ones 26. On 26 May 2020, during a turnaround at ENEOS’s Oita refinery, a fire in the structured packing of a 6.2 m diameter, 50 m tall crude column brought the column down about 70 minutes after the temperature rise was noticed 26.

  • Before opening, wash with an oxidising chemical (potassium permanganate is common) to passivate iron sulfide; steaming and water washing alone do not remove it 26.
  • Keep beds wet, monitor bed temperatures while manways are open, and have water ready to spray the packing 26.
  • Do not allow hot work above or below packing; new packing carries a film of lubricating oil that can burn too 26.
  • In fouling, sour services such as sour-water strippers, consider trays, which are easier to clean and less prone to metal fires 26.

Inherently safer first. Less inventory (packing holds less liquid than trays), lower temperature (vacuum), less hazardous solvents (no benzene as an entrainer), and designs that cannot overpressure (a heating medium too cool to exceed the design pressure) beat added protection.

Layers of protection. Basic control, alarms with operator response, an independent safety instrumented function, the relief valve, and the flare: each needs to be independent of the others to count.

A column needs 20 theoretical trays (excluding the reboiler). The keys have α = 1.9 and the liquid viscosity at the average temperature is 0.35 mPa·s. How many actual trays does O’Connell’s correlation give? Round up.

trays
E_o = 0.492 × (1.9 × 0.35)^−0.245 = 0.544; 20 / 0.544 = 36.8 → 37 trays

Problem 7. Flooding velocity at high pressure

From Fair’s chart CSB = 0.085 m/s. The liquid has ρL = 650 kg/m³ and σ = 15 mN/m; the vapour has ρV = 12 kg/m³. Find the flooding velocity on the net area (non-foaming, hole area 10 %).

m/s
u_nf = 0.085 × (15/20)^0.2 × √((650 − 12)/12) = 0.085 × 0.944 × 7.29 = 0.585 m/s

Dense vapour cuts the flooding velocity sharply; that is why high-pressure columns are wide for their mass flow.

Problem 8. Diameter from the vapour volume

A column carries 2.4 m³/s of vapour at a design velocity of 1.2 m/s on the net area. Downcomers take 12 % of the area. Find the diameter.

m
A_n = 2.4/1.2 = 2.00 m²; A_c = 2.00/0.88 = 2.27 m² D = √(4 × 2.27/π) = 1.70 m

Problem 9. Crest over the weir

A tray passes 12 kg/s of liquid with ρL = 780 kg/m³ over a 1.2 m weir. Find the weir crest.

mm
h_ow = 750 × [12/(780 × 1.2)]^(2/3) = 750 × 0.0548 = 41.1 mm of liquid

Problem 10. Dry tray pressure drop

Vapour passes the holes at 15 m/s with ρV = 3 kg/m³ over liquid of 800 kg/m³. The orifice coefficient is 0.84. Find the dry-tray head.

mm
h_d = 51 × (15/0.84)² × 3/800 = 51 × 318.9 × 0.00375 = 61.0 mm of liquid

The hole F-factor is 15 × √3 = 26 Pa0.5, comfortably above the weeping limits of equation (38).

Problem 11. Downcomer backup

A tray has hw = 50 mm, how = 12 mm, a total tray drop ht = 110 mm and a head loss under the downcomer hdc = 8 mm, all as liquid. The tray spacing is 600 mm. Find the clear-liquid backup hb.

mm
h_b = (50 + 12) + 110 + 8 = 180 mm; limit ½(600 + 50) = 325 mm → 55 % of the limit

Problem 12. Packed height

An organic separation needs 18 theoretical stages. Estimate the packed height with a Y-type structured packing of 250 m²/m³ using Kister’s rule.

m
HETP = 100/250 + 0.1 = 0.50 m; Z = 18 × 0.50 = 9.0 m

For an aqueous system the rule doubles the HETP to 1.0 m and the height to 18 m. Split long sections into beds with redistributors.

Problem 13. Pressure drop at flood in a packed bed

A packing has a packing factor of 18 ft⁻¹. Find the pressure drop at flood from Kister and Gill’s equation, in mbar per metre.

mbar/m
ΔP = 0.12 × 18^0.7 = 0.908 in. H₂O/ft × 83.3 = 75.6 mm H₂O/m × 0.0981 = 7.4 mbar/m

The equation is in US units; convert only at the end.

Problem 14. Relief load for loss of cooling

A column’s reboiler delivers 2.5 MW. The overhead vapour’s latent heat at relieving conditions is 300 kJ/kg. Estimate the relief load by the unbalanced-heat method, with no credits.

kg/h
W = 2,500 kW / 300 kJ/kg = 8.33 kg/s = 30,000 kg/h

If the heating medium is steam at a fixed temperature, the reboiler duty falls as the column temperature rises at relieving pressure; that “pinch” is a legitimate credit if it is calculated.

Problem 15. Shell thickness

A column has a design pressure of 1.2 MPa gauge and an inside diameter of 2,400 mm; SA-516-70 at 138 MPa, full radiography (E = 1.0), corrosion allowance 3 mm. Find the required thickness including the allowance.

mm
R = 1,200 + 3 = 1,203 mm t = 1.2 × 1,203 / (138 × 1.0 − 0.6 × 1.2) + 3 = 10.5 + 3 = 13.5 mm → order 14 mm or more

Problem 16. Cooling water for the condenser

A condenser removes 4.2 MW. Cooling water may rise 10 K (cp = 4.18 kJ/(kg·K)). Find the water flow.

kg/s
m = 4,200 / (4.18 × 10) = 100.5 kg/s ≈ 362 m³/h

Problem 17. Minimum boil-up in a stripper

A reboiled stripper with no reflux takes a saturated-liquid feed of zF = 0.20 on its top tray; α = 4. The bottoms must reach xB = 0.005. Find the minimum boil-up as V̄/F.

–
y*(0.20) = 0.8/1.6 = 0.500 (L̄/V̄)_max = (0.500 − 0.005)/(0.20 − 0.005) = 2.538 → (V̄/F)_min = 1/2.538 = 0.394

Problem 18. The hydrochloric acid azeotrope in mole fraction

Constant-boiling hydrochloric acid at 101.3 kPa is 20.2 wt % HCl. Convert to mole fraction HCl (M = 36.46 and 18.02).

–
x = (20.2/36.46) / (20.2/36.46 + 79.8/18.02) = 0.554/(0.554 + 4.430) = 0.111

A maximum-boiling azeotrope: distilling dilute acid sends water overhead and leaves the bottoms at this composition.

Problem 19. Which stream should hold the drum level?

A propylene–propane splitter runs at a reflux ratio L/D of 15 with the LV scheme of Figure 8: LIC-111 sets the distillate flow. At turndown the drum level swings and FV-110 works between 5 and 15 % open. What would you change on the P&ID, and what does the change do to the composition control?

The distillate is one sixteenth of the liquid leaving the drum, so a small error in the reflux flow is a large error in level, and the distillate valve works near its closed end, where it controls badly. Hold the level with the largest stream: move LIC-111 onto the reflux set point (the DV scheme). The distillate then becomes a composition handle, set by a rectifying-section temperature (TIC-120) or, on a splitter where the tray temperatures barely change, by the analyser AI-108 through a slow cascade.

The price is that reflux now moves only through the level loop, so LIC-111 must be tuned tight, and its tuning now affects how the column responds to composition moves. Select DV in Figure 8 to see the new cascades.

Problem 20. Spot the problem on the P&ID

A draft P&ID shows the column’s high-high pressure trip taking its signal from PT-101, the pressure controller’s transmitter, and closing FV-106, the steam control valve, through a solenoid. The PSV has been deleted “because the trip now protects the column”. List what is wrong and how you would fix it.

Three problems. The trip shares its transmitter with PIC-101: if PT-101 reads low, the controller lets the pressure rise and the trip never sees it. It shares its valve with the control loop: if FV-106 sticks open, nothing stops the steam. Neither counts as an independent layer.

The fix is the layout in Figure 8: a separate PT-116 into the SIS, logic in interlock I-100, and a separate XV-117 in series with FV-106, also closing the feed. Set the trip between the PIC high alarm and the relief set pressure. Keep the PSV: it is still required by the pressure-vessel code and is the last layer, sized for the governing API 521 case.

What goes on the column data sheet

The process data sheet is where a column design becomes an enquiry to the internals vendor and the vessel fabricator. Give every case that matters, not only the normal one.

  • Service and cases. Tag, service, and every case: normal, maximum, minimum (turndown), start-up and any alternate feeds.
  • Separation. Feed composition and condition; product specifications as purities and recoveries; the key components.
  • Stage requirements. Theoretical stages per section, feed and draw locations, assumed efficiency or HETP and its basis.
  • Loads per section. Top and bottom of each section: vapour and liquid mass flows, densities, viscosities, surface tension, molar masses, temperatures and pressures.
  • System behaviour. Foaming tendency and system factor, fouling, solids, polymerisation, corrosion, two liquid phases.
  • Internals. Tray type and number of passes or packing type; tray spacing; minimum turndown; allowable pressure drop; feed and draw devices; distributors.
  • Conditions. Operating and design pressure and temperature, minimum design metal temperature, vacuum design (full or partial).
  • Exchangers. Condenser and reboiler duties for each case, type (thermosiphon, kettle, fired), heating and cooling media.
  • Nozzles and levels. Nozzle schedule; sump hold-up; normal, alarm and trip levels; reboiler return elevation; independent level connections.
  • Control and instrumentation. Pressure-control method, which streams hold the levels, the temperature-control tray and spare thermowells, analyser sample points, and trip set points.
  • Relief. Set pressure and the governing case, with loss of cooling, loss of reflux, power failure and fire noted.
  • Materials. Shell, internals and packing materials; corrosion allowance; cladding; sour service.
  • Codes. ASME VIII (or the contract code), CSA B51 registration if installed in Canada, wind and seismic basis, owner standards.
